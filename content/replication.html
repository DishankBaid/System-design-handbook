Why it matters

A database on one machine is one disk failure away from losing data and one kernel upgrade away from downtime. Replication, keeping a copy of the same data on several machines, is how every serious system answers that. It is also where some of the most confusing bugs in distributed systems come from: a user saves their profile, refreshes, and the old version comes back.

Replication buys four things, and you should know which ones you are buying:

  • Durability. A committed write survives the loss of a disk, a machine or, with cross-region copies, a data centre.
  • Availability. When a node dies, another copy keeps serving.
  • Read throughput. Ten replicas can serve ten times the reads of one.
  • Latency. A copy in Frankfurt answers European users in 10 ms instead of 90 ms from Virginia.

The difficulty is never copying the data once. It is keeping copies in agreement while data changes, machines fail and networks drop messages. There are three families of answers, distinguished by who accepts writes: one leader, several leaders, or nobody in particular.

Single-leader replication

One replica is the leader (primary, master). All writes go to it. It applies each write locally and sends the change to the followers (replicas, secondaries, standbys), which apply changes in the same order. Reads can go to the leader or to any follower. PostgreSQL streaming replication, MySQL binlog replication, MongoDB replica sets, Redis replicas and Kafka partitions all work this way.

What travels to the followers varies:

Statement-based

Ship the SQL text. Compact, but NOW(), RAND() and auto-increment ordering can produce different results on each replica. Largely abandoned.

Physical (WAL shipping)

Ship the write-ahead log: byte-level page changes. Exact and cheap, but ties leader and followers to the same engine version. PostgreSQL streaming replication.

Logical (row-based)

Ship "row with id 42 changed from X to Y". Version-independent, readable by other systems, and the basis of change data capture. MySQL row-based binlog, PostgreSQL logical replication.

Synchronous, asynchronous and semi-synchronous

The central choice is when the leader tells the client "committed".

ModeCommit waits forLatencyLeader crashFollower down

Semi-synchronousLeader plus one (or k) followers confirmOne extra round trip to the fastest followerNothing lost, if the right follower is promotedFine unless all sync followers fail

In practice "synchronous" almost always means semi-synchronous. PostgreSQL's synchronous_standby_names = 'ANY 1 (b, c)' waits for any one of two standbys; synchronous_commit chooses whether that standby must have received, flushed or applied the record. MySQL's semi-sync plugin waits for one replica to acknowledge receipt, but silently falls back to asynchronous after a timeout (10 s by default). Within a region the extra round trip costs a millisecond or so, which is why most production setups use it for the data they cannot afford to lose. Across regions it costs 60 to 150 ms per commit, so cross-region replicas are usually asynchronous.

Replication lag

With asynchronous followers, a follower is always somewhat behind. Normally the replication lag is milliseconds. Under a write burst, a long-running query on the follower, a network hiccup or a replica rebuilding after a restart, it can grow to seconds or minutes. Any read served by a lagging follower returns data from the past.

This is eventual consistency: stop writing and all followers converge. It says nothing about how long that takes or what a client sees meanwhile. Three specific anomalies matter in practice, and each has a targeted fix that is much cheaper than making every read go to the leader.

GuaranteeAnomaly without itCommon fixes
Read-your-writesYou save a change, reload, and it is goneRead your own recently changed data from the leader (e.g. for 10 s after a write); or remember the log position of your last write and read only from a follower that has replayed past it
Monotonic readsA comment appears, you refresh, it disappears, it reappears: consecutive reads hit followers with different lagPin each user to one follower (hash the user id); fail over to another only if it dies
Consistent prefixYou see an answer before the question it replies to, because they live on different partitions with different lagKeep causally related writes in the same partition, or track causal dependencies

The log-position approach is precise and cheap. After committing, the client reads the leader's WAL position (pg_current_wal_lsn()); a follower reports how far it has replayed (pg_last_wal_replay_lsn()). MySQL offers the same with GTIDs and WAIT_FOR_EXECUTED_GTID_SET. The client sends its last-seen position as a token, and the routing layer picks a follower that has caught up, or waits briefly. These session guarantees are defined in Consistency Models.

Failover, split brain and fencing

Follower failure is easy: it reconnects and catches up from the position it last applied. Leader failure needs failover: detect the failure, choose a new leader, and redirect clients and the remaining followers to it. Each step has a sharp edge.

  • Detection is a guess. A node that does not answer may be dead, overloaded, in a 30-second garbage collection pause or on the wrong side of a network partition. A short timeout causes needless failovers under load (which add load); a long one means minutes of downtime. Values of 10 to 30 seconds are common.
  • Lost writes. With asynchronous replication, writes the old leader committed but had not yet shipped disappear when a follower is promoted. Worse, if the old leader comes back and those writes are replayed, they conflict with new ones. In GitHub's 2012 incident, a promoted MySQL follower that was behind reused auto-increment ids that were also used as keys in Redis, so some users saw other users' private data.
  • Split brain. If the old leader was only paused, it may resume and keep accepting writes. The standard defence is a fencing token: a number that increases with every leadership change (an epoch, term or generation), issued by a consensus service such as etcd or ZooKeeper. Every write carries it, and storage or replicas reject any token lower than the highest they have seen. Some systems also use STONITH ("shoot the other node in the head"), powering off the old leader through its management interface before promoting the new one.
  • Choosing the new leader is a consensus problem: nodes must agree on one winner even with failures. Use a proven implementation (Patroni with etcd for PostgreSQL, Orchestrator for MySQL, Raft in MongoDB and Kafka's KRaft). See Consensus & Leader Election.

Multi-leader replication

With multi-leader (active-active) replication, several nodes accept writes and replicate to each other asynchronously. The typical setup is one leader per region: users write locally with low latency, and a region can keep working while cut off from the others. Offline-capable clients are the same pattern at a smaller scale: a phone's calendar is a leader that syncs later. Collaborative editors are the extreme case, with every keystroke a write on a different leader.

The cost is write conflicts. If a user changes a title to "Q3 plan" in Europe while a colleague changes it to "Q3 roadmap" in the US, both writes succeed locally, and when they replicate the database has two values and no single order to decide between them. Options, in order of preference:

  1. Avoid conflicts. Give each record a home region and route all its writes there (a user's data lives in their home region). Conflicts then only arise during failover of a region. Most production multi-region systems do this.
  2. Last write wins (LWW). Attach a timestamp to each write and keep the highest. Simple and convergent, but it silently discards the other write, and with clock skew between regions it can discard the later one. Acceptable for caches and "last seen" fields, not for anything a user typed.
  3. Merge in the application. Keep both versions ("siblings") and resolve on read or with custom logic: union two shopping carts, ask the user. Amazon's Dynamo cart did exactly this.
  4. CRDTs. Conflict-free replicated data types are counters, sets, maps and text sequences designed so that concurrent updates always merge to the same result regardless of order. Riak, Redis Enterprise's active-active mode and collaborative editing libraries such as Automerge and Yjs use them.

Multi-leader also breaks database features that assume one order of writes: auto-increment ids, uniqueness constraints and foreign keys cannot be checked across leaders at write time. Treat it as a tool for specific needs (multi-region writes, offline clients), not as a default.

Leaderless replication

Amazon's Dynamo paper (2007) popularised a design with no leader at all; Cassandra, ScyllaDB, Riak and Voldemort follow it. The client (or a coordinator node acting for it) sends each write to all N replicas of the key and considers it successful when W of them acknowledge. A read asks N replicas, waits for R answers, and returns the value with the newest version. The replicas for a key are chosen by walking the hash ring, as described in Consistent Hashing.

Quorums: R + W > N

If R + W > N, every read set overlaps every write set in at least one node, so at least one of the replies carries the latest acknowledged write. That is the quorum condition. With N = 3, the usual choice is W = 2, R = 2: one node can be down for both reads and writes.

The counting argument is easy to check by brute force, and the same script gives the chance of a stale read when the condition does not hold. It assumes the worst moment: the write has reached exactly W replicas and nothing else yet.

import itertools
import random
from math import comb


def p_stale_exact(n, w, r):
    """Chance a read of r random replicas misses all w replicas that acked the write
    (assumes the write reached exactly those w nodes and nothing else yet)."""
    return comb(n - w, r) / comb(n, r)


def p_stale_simulated(n, w, r, trials=100_000, seed=7):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    nodes = range(n)
    stale = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        has_new = set(rng.sample(nodes, w))
        read_set = rng.sample(nodes, r)
        stale += not has_new.intersection(read_set)
    return stale / trials


print(" N  W  R  R+W>N  exact   simulated")
for n, w, r in [(3, 1, 1), (3, 2, 1), (3, 2, 2), (5, 2, 2), (5, 3, 2), (5, 3, 3)]:
    print(f"{n:2} {w:2} {r:2}  {str(r + w > n):5}  {p_stale_exact(n, w, r):.3f}   {p_stale_simulated(n, w, r):.3f}")

# Exhaustive check: every possible write set and read set overlap iff R + W > N.
for n in range(1, 8):
    for w, r in itertools.product(range(1, n + 1), repeat=2):
        always = all(set(ws) & set(rs) for ws in itertools.combinations(range(n), w)
                     for rs in itertools.combinations(range(n), r))
        assert always == (r + w > n)
print("overlap guaranteed exactly when R + W > N, checked for N = 1..7")

#  N  W  R  R+W>N  exact   simulated
#  3  1  1  False  0.667   0.667
#  3  2  1  False  0.333   0.334
#  3  2  2  True   0.000   0.000
#  5  2  2  False  0.300   0.300
#  5  3  2  False  0.100   0.101
#  5  3  3  True   0.000   0.000
# overlap guaranteed exactly when R + W > N, checked for N = 1..7

Real stale-read rates are far lower than the worst case, because replicas that were not in the write quorum usually receive the write milliseconds later anyway. Studies of Dynamo-style systems (the Probabilistically Bounded Staleness work from Berkeley) found that even R = W = 1 reads usually return the latest write once a fraction of a second has passed. "Usually" is the point: it is a probability, not a guarantee.

What quorums do not give you

R + W > N guarantees overlap, not linearizability. Several edge cases return stale or inconsistent data even with strict quorums:

  • Writes in flight. While a write has reached one of three replicas, one quorum read may see the new value and a later quorum read may not. To prevent that, the reader must write the newest value back to a quorum before returning (the ABD algorithm), which costs a second round trip.
  • Partial failures are not rolled back. If a write reaches one replica and then fails to reach W, the client gets an error, but the value stays on that replica and may later spread through repair. The "failed" write can win.
  • Concurrent writes with last-write-wins. Cassandra resolves conflicts per column by timestamp; with clock skew, a write that happened later can lose.
  • Sloppy quorums. If the write was accepted by stand-in nodes, the overlap argument no longer applies (step 3 above).
  • A replica restored from an old backup or rebuilt from a stale copy can drop below the latest value and break the count.

Leaderless stores are a good fit for high-availability, write-heavy workloads that tolerate eventual consistency. When you need compare-and-set, Cassandra offers lightweight transactions that run Paxos per partition, at several times the latency.

Sloppy quorums and hinted handoff

When some of a key's home replicas are unreachable, a strict quorum fails the write. A sloppy quorum instead accepts the write on other healthy nodes, which store it with a hint naming the intended owner. When the owner comes back, hinted handoff delivers the write to it. Dynamo used this so a shopping cart would always accept items. Cassandra stores hints for a bounded window (three hours by default) but does not count them towards the consistency level (except the weakest level, ANY), so its quorums stay strict.

Read repair and anti-entropy

Replicas drift apart: missed writes during outages, hints that expired, dropped messages. Two mechanisms pull them back together.

  • Read repair: when a read sees different versions from different replicas, it writes the newest version back to the stale ones. Cheap, but only repairs keys that are read.
  • Anti-entropy: a background process compares replicas and copies missing data. Comparing every key would mean shipping the whole dataset, so replicas compare Merkle trees instead: hash trees where each leaf hashes a range of keys and each parent hashes its children. Equal roots mean equal data. Unequal roots send the comparison down only the branches that differ.

Worked example: tuning N, W and R

A user-profile store runs Cassandra with N = 3, one replica in each of three availability zones. Profile reads run at 50,000 per second, writes at 2,000 per second. The latency of a request is roughly the latency of the slowest replica you wait for, so waiting for 2 of 3 is cheap (the second-fastest of three), and waiting for 3 of 3 means every slow replica hurts the tail. For profiles, QUORUM reads and writes are the right default: users edit their own profile and expect to see the change. Each profile read touches two replicas, so the cluster serves 100,000 replica reads per second plus 6,000 replica writes (every write goes to all three; the client waits for two). Across regions, use LOCAL_QUORUM so a request waits only for replicas in its own region and the other regions receive the write asynchronously.

In the real world

  • PostgreSQL ships WAL to hot standbys; Patroni with etcd handles leader election and fencing for most self-managed deployments. Logical replication feeds change data capture tools such as Debezium.
  • Amazon Aurora replicates the storage layer, not the database: six copies across three availability zones, with writes needing 4 of 6 and reads 3 of 6, so it survives losing a whole zone plus one more node for reads.
  • MongoDB replica sets elect a primary with a Raft-like protocol; writeConcern: "majority" makes a write survive failover, and readConcern controls whether reads can see writes that might still be rolled back.
  • Kafka keeps an in-sync replica set per partition; with acks=all and min.insync.replicas=2, a produce succeeds only once every in-sync replica has it, and is rejected if fewer than two replicas are in sync.
  • Cassandra exposes the quorum knobs per query (ONE, QUORUM, LOCAL_QUORUM, ALL), which makes it the clearest place to see these trade-offs in production.
Interview angle

When to bring it up

Whenever you draw a database box, say how it is replicated in one sentence: "PostgreSQL primary with one synchronous standby in another zone and async read replicas; reads that must see the user's own writes go to the primary." In a key-value store design, leaderless replication with quorums is the core of the answer.

Follow-ups to expect

  • What happens to writes in flight when the leader dies? (Async: lost; semi-sync: safe if the right follower is promoted.)
  • A user updates their profile and sees the old one. Why, and how do you fix it cheaply?
  • How do you prevent split brain? (Consensus-based election plus fencing tokens.)
  • With N = 3, which W and R give strong reads, and is that linearizable? (W = R = 2 overlap; not linearizable on its own.)
  • How do replicas that missed writes catch up? (Hinted handoff, read repair, Merkle-tree anti-entropy.)

Red flags

  • Adding read replicas without mentioning lag or read-your-writes.
  • Claiming R + W > N makes the system strongly consistent.
  • Automatic failover with no fencing.
  • Multi-leader replication for data with uniqueness constraints, without a conflict strategy.
