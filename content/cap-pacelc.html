Why it matters

A database replicated across two regions loses the link between them. A user in Frankfurt tries to update a record. The Frankfurt replica cannot reach Virginia. It has two options: accept the write and risk disagreeing with Virginia, or refuse it and tell the user to try later. There is no third option, and whichever one the system picks is a product decision someone should have made on purpose.

That forced choice is the CAP theorem. It is the most quoted and most misquoted result in distributed systems. Interviewers use it to check whether you can reason precisely about failure, rather than recite "pick two of three". This chapter states CAP exactly, shows what CP and AP systems actually do during a partition, extends it with PACELC to cover the much more common case when nothing is broken, and classifies real systems with the caveats that classification needs.

The precise statement

Eric Brewer stated the conjecture in a keynote at PODC in 2000. Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch proved it in 2002, and their proof pins down what each letter means. The definitions are narrower than everyday usage, and most confusion comes from ignoring them.

C: consistency

Linearizability: every operation appears to take effect at a single instant between its start and end, so all clients see one up-to-date copy. A read never returns a value older than a write that completed before the read began. See Consistency Models.

A: availability

Every request received by a non-failing node eventually gets a non-error response. Not "the service is up" or "99.99%": every working node must answer, with no time bound.

P: partition tolerance

The system keeps its guarantees even when the network loses arbitrarily many messages between nodes. A network partition splits nodes into groups that cannot talk to each other.

The theorem: in a network that can lose messages, no system can guarantee both linearizability and availability. The proof is short. Put two replicas on opposite sides of a partition. A client writes to one; another client then reads from the other. To be available, the second replica must answer. It cannot know about the write, so it must return the old value, which breaks linearizability. To stay linearizable, it must refuse to answer, which breaks availability.

What a partition looks like in practice

Partitions are not only cut cables. Any of these produces one from the system's point of view:

  • A failed switch or a misconfigured firewall rule isolating a rack or a zone.
  • A congested or flapping link between regions, dropping or delaying packets for minutes.
  • A long garbage-collection pause or a VM freeze: the node is alive but silent, and when it wakes it may still believe it is the leader.
  • Asymmetric failures, where A can reach B but B cannot reach A.

The crucial property is that a node cannot tell a partition from a crash or from a slow network. All it sees is a missing reply. A timeout is therefore a decision point: give up and answer with what you have (choose A), or keep waiting or return an error (choose C). Large studies of production incidents, such as Bailis and Kingsbury's survey of network partitions, found them to be a regular occurrence in real datacenters, not a theoretical edge case.

Why "pick two of three" is misleading

The popular triangle with C, A and P at the corners suggests three equal options: CA, CP and AP. That framing is wrong in several ways.

  1. P is not optional. You cannot choose to have no partitions in a system that uses a network. "CA" only describes a single-node system, or a cluster that silently breaks its guarantees when a partition happens. The real choice is what to give up when a partition occurs: C or A.
  2. The choice only applies during a partition. When the network is healthy, a system can be both linearizable and available. CAP says nothing about normal operation, which is where systems spend nearly all their time. PACELC, below, fills that gap.
  3. The choice can be made per operation. A shop can keep browsing and cart updates available while making checkout consistent. Cassandra lets each request pick its consistency level; DynamoDB lets each read choose eventual or strong.
  4. C and A in CAP are extreme definitions. Most databases offer neither linearizability nor CAP-availability. A single-leader database with asynchronous replicas is not linearizable (replica reads can be stale) and not CAP-available (a partitioned-off replica cannot accept writes). Calling it "CP" or "AP" says little. Martin Kleppmann's essay "Please stop calling databases CP or AP" makes this argument in detail.
  5. Latency is the hidden variable. A system that answers after 30 seconds is technically available. In practice, users treat a very slow answer as no answer, so the real trade-off is between consistency and response time.

CP and AP behaviour during a partition

Both systems below have three replicas: N1 and N2 in us-east, N3 in eu-west. Clients write a key x. Step through the same failure for each design.

CP (consistent under partition)AP (available under partition)
Minority sideRejects writes and linearizable reads, or times outKeeps serving reads and writes
Majority sideKeeps servingKeeps serving
ReadsNever stale if served by the leader or a quorum; local follower reads (e.g. ZooKeeper) can beMay be stale, or reflect writes later discarded

Conflict handlingNot neededLast-writer-wins, version vectors with siblings, or CRDTs

Note that a CP system is not "down" during a partition. The majority side keeps working; only clients that can reach only the minority are refused. With three or five replicas spread across zones, that is usually a small slice of traffic. Coordination services such as etcd and ZooKeeper work exactly this way, and so do the leader-election mechanisms described in Consensus & Leader Election.

AP systems need a reconciliation strategy, and this is where the real design work lies:

  • Last-writer-wins (LWW) keeps the write with the highest timestamp. Simple, and silently discards the other write. Also vulnerable to clock skew between nodes.
  • Siblings: detect concurrent writes with version vectors and return all versions to the application to merge, as Amazon's Dynamo did for shopping carts.
  • CRDTs (conflict-free replicated data types): counters, sets and maps whose merge is mathematically guaranteed to converge, such as a set where concurrent adds are all kept.

The Replication chapter covers quorums and conflict resolution in depth; the key-value store case study builds an AP store end to end.

PACELC: the trade-off when nothing is broken

Daniel Abadi pointed out in 2010, and formalised in a 2012 paper, that CAP ignores the trade-off that shapes systems every day. Partitions are rare; replication latency is constant. His PACELC formulation reads: if there is a Partition, choose between Availability and Consistency; Else, choose between Latency and Consistency.

The else-branch cost is concrete. Consistency across replicas requires coordinating with them before answering, and coordination costs round trips:

Same zone round trip ~0.5 ms consistency is nearly free Across zones round trip ~1-2 ms usually worth paying us-east ↔ eu-west round trip ~70-90 ms paid by every consistent write us-east ↔ ap-south round trip ~180+ ms Majority of 3 regions: each write waits for the nearest other region

This is why globally distributed systems so often choose EL: a social app that made every like wait 80 ms for another continent would feel sluggish, while a like that takes a second to appear elsewhere bothers nobody. And it is why systems that do choose EC across regions, such as Spanner, invest heavily in reducing the cost (placing leaders near writers, using TrueTime to serve consistent reads locally).

Worked example: shopping cart vs bank balance

The same partition, two products, opposite answers. Assume two regions and a partition lasting ten minutes during peak.

Shopping cart: choose AP

A customer adds a book in one session served by us-east and a lamp in another served by eu-west. Rejecting either add loses a sale; both are low-risk. The cart is AP, and concurrent versions are merged by union, which is exactly the design described in the Dynamo paper.

us-east version {book} eu-west version {lamp} merge on heal {book} ∪ {lamp} = {book, lamp} nothing lost anomaly an item removed on one side can reappear after merge cost of anomaly the customer deletes it again, or notices at checkout

The anomaly is real but cheap, and checkout can re-validate the cart against current stock and prices.

Bank balance: choose CP, with a bounded escape hatch

An account holds $100. During the partition, the account holder withdraws $80 at an ATM routed to us-east and, at the same moment, someone with the joint card withdraws $80 routed to eu-west.

APCPCP with a partition limit
us-east withdrawalApprovedApproved (majority side)Approved up to $40 per side
eu-west withdrawalApprovedRejected: "try again later"Approved up to $40
Balance after heal-$60 overdraft$20$20; never below zero
Customer experienceGood now, angry bank laterOne failed withdrawalBoth get some cash

The third column is the design Brewer describes for ATMs in "CAP Twelve Years Later": during a partition the system stays available for bounded operations and limits the damage, then reconciles and applies compensation (an overdraft fee, a reversal) afterwards. That is the mature answer in an interview: not "banks are CP", but "money movement is CP by default, with explicitly bounded availability during partitions where the business accepts the risk".

Designing for partition mode

Brewer's 2012 follow-up recasts CAP as a process rather than a one-time label. A well-designed system plans for three phases:

  1. Detect the partition. In practice, a timeout on a call to another replica or region. Choosing the timeout is choosing how long you will sacrifice availability before you decide a partition exists. Too short and you flap into partition mode on every latency spike; too long and users wait.
  2. Enter partition mode. Decide per operation what is allowed. Reads of the cart: allowed. Adding items: allowed. Charging a card: queued, or allowed only up to a limit. Changing a username, which must be globally unique: refused. Record every operation done in this mode so it can be reconciled.
  3. Recover. When communication returns, merge the two histories: replay logged operations, merge CRDT state, detect invariant violations (two users with the same username, a negative balance) and fix them with compensating actions such as refunds, apologies or a manual review queue.

Thinking in these phases changes the interview conversation from "which letter do we drop" to "which operations stay available, what invariant might break, and how do we repair it". That is how real systems such as airline booking (overbooking, then compensation) and payment networks (offline approvals under a limit) have always worked.

Common misconceptions

MisconceptionReality
"Our system is CA."Only a single node is CA. A distributed system that claims CA has not decided what happens during a partition, which means it does something unplanned.
"CP means the system goes down during a partition."Only the minority side stops serving. The majority keeps working.
"AP means no consistency at all."AP systems usually provide eventual consistency and often session guarantees such as read-your-writes. They give up linearizability, not all ordering.
"The C in CAP is the C in ACID."ACID consistency means preserving application invariants within a transaction. CAP consistency means linearizability across replicas. See ACID & Isolation Levels.
"Availability in CAP is the same as uptime nines."CAP availability means every non-failing node answers. Real availability is measured as a fraction of successful requests; see Latency, Throughput & Availability.
"We picked a CP database, so our application is consistent."Caches, read replicas, async queues and client retries in front of it can each reintroduce stale reads and duplicates.
"Partitions are rare, so CAP does not matter."Partitions are rare; the latency trade-off (PACELC's else branch) applies to every request.
Interview angle

When to bring it up

When you replicate data across zones or regions, state per data type what happens during a partition and what consistency normal reads get. "Cart and likes are AP with merge; payments and inventory decrements are CP; the feed reads from the nearest replica (EL)." Avoid a lecture on the theorem unless asked.

Follow-ups to expect

  • "What exactly does the C mean?" Linearizability, with an example of a stale read it forbids.
  • "Is this database CP or AP?" Answer with configuration: consistency levels, write concern, sync vs async replication.
  • "How do you resolve conflicts in the AP path?" LWW, version vectors with siblings, or CRDTs, and what each loses.
  • "What does consistency cost when there is no partition?" Round trips to replicas: give cross-region numbers.
  • "How does a node know there is a partition?" It does not; it sees timeouts, and the timeout is the decision.

Red flags

  • Choosing "CA" for a distributed design.
  • Saying "pick two of three" without explaining that the choice only arises during a partition.
  • Labelling a database CP or AP with no reference to how it is configured.
  • Confusing CAP consistency with ACID consistency or with transaction isolation.
