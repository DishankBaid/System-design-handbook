Why it matters

A system design interview gives you 45 to 60 minutes, a one-line prompt such as "design Instagram", and no right answer. Candidates rarely fail because they do not know what a cache is. They fail because they spend 25 minutes on requirements, or draw boxes before knowing what the boxes are for, or never get to the part where they show judgement.

The interview is a simulation of a design review with a colleague. The interviewer wants to see how you turn ambiguity into a concrete plan, which risks you notice, and how you reason about trade-offs under time pressure. A repeatable structure frees your attention for that reasoning. This chapter gives you the structure, the time budget, what interviewers write down at each level, and the habits that sink otherwise strong candidates.

Everything else in this handbook plugs into this framework. Estimation fills the second phase. The Data and Async parts supply the components for the high-level design. The case studies, starting with the URL shortener, are worked runs of the whole thing.

The time budget

Most loops use a 45-minute slot (after introductions, closer to 40) or a 60-minute slot. The phases are the same either way. What changes is how long you can afford to go deep.

Phase45 min60 minYou leave it with
1. Requirements573 to 5 functional requirements, the non-functional priorities, what is out of scope
2. Estimation45Read and write QPS, storage per year, one or two conclusions drawn from them
3. API45The 3 to 5 endpoints that serve the functional requirements
4. Data model45Core entities, their keys, the main access patterns, a storage choice
5. High-level design1013A diagram that serves every functional requirement end to end
6. Deep dives1420Two or three hard parts solved, with alternatives and trade-offs stated
7. Wrap-up45Bottlenecks, failure modes, what you would do next

Treat the budget as a guard rail, not a script. If the interviewer interrupts at minute 8 with "skip the numbers, assume it is big", skip them. If they want to spend 20 minutes on one component, follow them. The budget matters most when nobody steers, because then the default failure is running out of time before the deep dives.

Some companies merge or reorder phases

Many interviewers now say "skip estimation unless a number changes your design". Others want the API before requirements are finished, or the data model folded into the high-level design. The phases still happen, just compressed. Ask early: "I usually go requirements, a quick estimate, API, then design. Does that work for you?"

From vague to concrete

Each phase narrows the problem. The prompt allows thousands of designs. Requirements cut that to hundreds, numbers to dozens, interfaces to a handful, and the high-level design to one picture you can then stress. Skipping a level means making the next level's decisions without information.

Phase 1: requirements

The goal is a short, written list that you and the interviewer agree on. Write it on the board. It becomes the checklist you verify the design against at the end.

Functional vs non-functional

Functional requirements

What the system does, phrased as user actions. "A user can upload a photo with a caption." "A user sees a feed of recent posts from people they follow." Each one should map to an API call later.

Non-functional requirements

How well it does it: latency, availability, consistency, durability, scale, cost, security. "Feed loads in under 500 ms at p99." "An uploaded photo is never lost." These drive almost every architectural choice.

Out of scope

Features you name and deliberately park: stories, direct messages, ads, search. Saying them out loud shows you know they exist, and protects your time.

A functional requirement describes behaviour. A non-functional requirement describes a quality the behaviour must have. Weak candidates list functional requirements and stop. Strong candidates spend most of the phase on the non-functional side, because two systems with identical features (a bank ledger and a social feed both "show a list of recent items") end up with opposite designs once you ask how stale the list may be.

Questions that change the design

Every question should be one whose answer moves a box. "What colour is the button?" does not. These do:

QuestionWhy it mattersDesign it changes
Who are the users and how many?Sets every later numberSingle database vs sharded, one region vs many
What is the read/write ratio?Read-heavy systems cache and precompute; write-heavy ones batch and partitionFan-out on write vs read, cache vs log
How fresh must reads be?Decides whether replicas and caches may serve slightly stale dataConsistency model, replication mode (see Consistency Models)
What happens if we lose data?A lost like is fine, a lost payment is notSynchronous replication, durable queues, idempotency
What latency do users feel?p99 targets bound how many hops and disk reads a request may takeCaching, precomputation, regional deployment
Global or one region?Cross-region round trips are roughly 60 to 250 ms (US-Europe ~70-90 ms, US-Asia ~150-250 ms)Data placement, multi-region writes, CDN
Common mistake

Asking twenty questions and getting no decisions. If the interviewer answers "you decide", decide, say the assumption out loud, and write it down: "I'll assume 500 million daily users and that a feed may be up to a minute stale." A stated assumption is a decision the interviewer can redirect. An open question is just lost time.

Phase 2: estimation

Estimate only what will drive a decision: requests per second for reads and writes, storage growth, and sometimes bandwidth or cache size. Round aggressively. A day has 86,400 seconds; use 100,000 and say so. Then draw a conclusion from every number, because a number with no consequence is wasted time.

Feed reads 500M DAU × 10 opens/day = 5B/day ÷ ~10^5 s ≈ 50k/s (exact 58k, peak ~2×) Uploads 100M photos/day ÷ ~10^5 s ≈ 1k/s (exact 1.2k) Media 100M × ~2 MB (original + resized) ≈ 200 TB/day ≈ 73 PB/year Conclusion reads outnumber writes ~50:1 → precompute and cache the feed media dwarfs metadata → object store + CDN, not the database

The Back-of-Envelope Estimation chapter covers the techniques and the reference numbers in detail.

Phases 3 and 4: API and data model

The API turns each functional requirement into a call with inputs and outputs. Keep it to the few endpoints that matter and state the non-obvious details: pagination with a cursor rather than an offset, idempotency keys on writes that clients retry, and who is authenticated. Detail on these choices lives in API Design.

POST /v1/posts            { caption, media_ids[] }      → 201 { post_id }
POST /v1/media            (returns a presigned upload URL)
GET  /v1/feed?cursor=...  → 200 { posts[20], next_cursor }
POST /v1/follows          { followee_id }               → 204

For the data model, list the core entities, their primary keys, and above all the access patterns: "get the 20 newest posts in a user's feed" is a different storage problem from "get one post by id". Then pick a store that serves those patterns. The choice should follow from the patterns, which Data Modeling and SQL vs NoSQL cover. One or two sentences of justification is enough here; you will return to it in deep dives if it matters.

Phase 5: the high-level design

Draw the simplest diagram that serves every functional requirement, then walk one request through it end to end. Start simple on purpose. A design that grows in response to stated requirements is far more convincing than one that starts with fifteen boxes, because every box then has a reason you can point to.

Narrate the evolution rather than presenting v3 as if it fell from the sky. "This works, but the feed query joins follows with posts for every request. At 58,000 reads per second and a 500 ms target that will not hold, so I'll precompute feeds." The interviewer now knows why the cache and queue exist, and can push on that reasoning instead of on the boxes.

  • Walk a request through it. "The app calls GET /v1/feed, the load balancer routes to any feed service instance, which reads 20 post ids from the cache and hydrates them." Walking the path catches missing boxes.
  • Label the edges, not just the boxes. Synchronous call, async event, or batch job. Which arrows are on the hot path.
  • Check against the requirement list. Point at each functional requirement and show which path serves it.

Phase 6: deep dives

This is the largest block of time and the one that decides senior and staff outcomes. Pick the two or three parts of the design that are genuinely hard for this problem, and say why you picked them. For Instagram: feed generation for users who follow celebrities, media upload and delivery, and ID generation for posts that sort by time. For a payment system it would be idempotency and exactly-once ledger updates instead.

Often the interviewer chooses. If they do not, offer a menu: "The interesting parts are feed fan-out, the media pipeline and the hot-key problem for celebrities. I'd start with fan-out. Sound good?"

Every deep dive follows the same shape:

  1. State the problem precisely. "A celebrity with 50 million followers posts. Fan-out on write means 50 million cache writes for one post."
  2. Offer two or three options. Fan-out on write, fan-out on read, or a hybrid.
  3. Compare them on the requirements. Write amplification, read latency, freshness, storage. Use numbers when you have them.
  4. Choose, and say what you gave up. "Hybrid: push for normal accounts, pull for accounts above about 100,000 followers, merged at read time. We pay extra complexity in the read path."
  5. Name the failure mode. What happens when the fan-out queue backs up? Feeds go stale, posts still save. That is the right way round for this product.

Concept chapters such as Caching, Sharding, Message Queues and Timeouts, Retries & Circuit Breakers are the raw material for deep dives. The News Feed case study runs the fan-out deep dive in full.

Phase 7: wrap-up

Reserve the last few minutes, even if the interviewer seems happy to keep going. Use them to show that you can judge your own design:

  • Bottlenecks: "The first thing to break is the hot shard holding a viral post's like counter."
  • Failure modes: what happens when a region, the cache cluster or the queue goes down, and what users see.
  • Operations: the two or three metrics you would alert on, and how you would roll out a schema change.
  • Next steps: the parked features and how the design would absorb them.

Driving the conversation

The interviewer is grading whether you can lead a design discussion, so lead it. That does not mean talking non-stop. It means always making clear where you are, where you are going next, and inviting redirection at natural points.

Signpost

"I have the requirements. Next I'll do a two-minute estimate, mainly to size the feed reads." The interviewer always knows the plan and can change it cheaply.

Think out loud

Silence gives the interviewer nothing to grade. Say the options you are weighing, even half-formed: "I'm deciding between a queue and a log here. The question is whether anything else needs to replay these events."

Check in at boundaries

At the end of each phase: "Anything you'd like me to change before I go deeper?" Not after every sentence, which reads as insecurity.

Treat hints as gifts

"What happens if that service is slow?" is rarely idle curiosity. It usually points at a weakness. Answer it directly, then fix the design.

The interviewer's questions also tell you what they care about. If they ask twice about consistency, the deep dive should be about consistency, whatever you had planned.

What interviewers grade, by level

Rubrics differ by company but nearly all score the same handful of dimensions: problem navigation (scoping and prioritising), solution design, technical depth, trade-off reasoning, and communication. What changes with level is how much of it you are expected to do without help.

DimensionMid-level (L4, SDE II)Senior (L5, Senior SDE)Staff (L6+)
ScopingGathers requirements when prompted; may miss a key non-functional oneDrives scoping unprompted; identifies the requirement that dominates the designQuestions the problem itself; spots business constraints and cost before being asked
DesignA working end-to-end design with standard componentsA design fitted to this problem's numbers; every component justifiedSeveral viable designs compared; chooses one with a clear argument about evolution over years
DepthExplains how a cache or a queue worksGoes deep on two areas unprompted: failure handling, consistency, hot spotsDeep in the areas that carry the most risk, including operations, migrations, and multi-team boundaries
Trade-offsNames a trade-off when askedStates what each choice gives up, with numbersWeighs trade-offs against organisational cost: on-call load, team ownership, build vs buy
DrivingFollows the interviewer's lead wellLeads the session; uses hints quicklyLeads, and steers toward the parts that matter most; the interviewer mostly listens

The same answer can pass at one level and fail at another. "Put a cache in front of the database" is fine at mid-level. At senior the interviewer expects you to say what is cached, the hit rate you expect, how entries are invalidated, and what happens to the database when the cache cluster restarts cold.

Handling "I don't know"

You will hit a question you cannot answer: the internals of a database you have never used, an exact number, an algorithm you half remember. Bluffing is the worst response, because interviewers often probe precisely where they expect bluffing, and a confident wrong answer damages trust in everything else you said. Instead:

  1. Say what you do know. "I haven't operated Spanner, but I know it uses synchronised clocks with bounded uncertainty to order transactions globally."
  2. Reason from first principles. "If I had to build that, I'd need some way to agree on order across regions, so I'd expect a commit to cost at least one cross-region round trip."
  3. Bound the estimate. "I don't know the exact write throughput of one Kafka partition. It is sequential disk I/O, so I'd assume tens of megabytes per second and verify with a benchmark."
  4. Make it a decision point. "Whichever it is, I'd design so that this component can be swapped, and here is the interface."

Interviewers grade reasoning more than recall. "I don't know, but here is how I'd find out and here is what I'd assume until then" is a strong answer at every level.

Worked example: the first 10 minutes of "Design Instagram"

Here is how the opening of a 45-minute session can sound. Notice that the candidate makes a decision after almost every exchange, and that each decision appears later in the design.

TimeWhoWhat is said
0:00InterviewerLet's design Instagram.
0:10CandidateInstagram is big, so I'd like to pick the core. I'm thinking: upload a photo with a caption, follow other users, and see a home feed of posts from people I follow. I'd park stories, direct messages, search, comments and ads. Does that match what you want?
0:40InterviewerYes. Include likes, but only the count.
0:50CandidateGot it, a like count per post. Now scale: should I assume something like today's Instagram, hundreds of millions of daily users?
1:05InterviewerSay 500 million daily active users.
1:15CandidateOn quality: I'd assume the feed must load fast, say under 500 ms at p99, and that it may be slightly stale. If someone I follow posts, seeing it a few seconds or even a minute later is fine. Is that acceptable?
1:40InterviewerFine. What about the photos themselves?
1:50CandidateUploaded photos must never be lost, so durability matters more for media than for likes. A lost like is acceptable; a lost photo is not. Like counts can be approximate for a few seconds. I'll write these down.
2:30Candidate(writes) Functional: post photo, follow, home feed, like count. Non-functional: feed p99 < 500 ms, feed eventually consistent, media durable, highly available reads. Out: stories, DMs, search, comments, ads.
3:30CandidateQuick numbers. If an average user opens the feed 10 times a day, that is 5 billion feed reads a day. With about 105 seconds in a day that is roughly 50,000 per second, call it 100,000 at peak. If 1 in 5 users posts once a day, that is 100 million uploads, about 1,000 per second.
5:00CandidateSo reads outnumber writes about 50 to 1. That tells me to do work at write time to make reads cheap. Media: 100 million photos at roughly 2 MB including resized versions is 200 TB a day. That goes in an object store behind a CDN, never in the database.
6:10InterviewerGood. Keep going.
6:20CandidateAPI. Upload is two steps: the client asks for a presigned URL and uploads bytes straight to the object store, then calls POST /v1/posts with the caption and media id. That keeps 200 TB a day out of our servers. The feed is GET /v1/feed?cursor= returning 20 posts, cursor-based so new posts at the top don't shift pages. Plus follow and like endpoints.
7:40CandidateData: users, posts keyed by a time-sortable post id, follows as (follower, followee) pairs that I need to query in both directions, and a per-user feed list of post ids. Posts and follows are simple key lookups at huge scale, so a sharded key-value or wide-column store fits. I'll come back to that if we deep dive.
8:50CandidateLet me start the diagram with the simplest version and grow it. (draws v1, then explains why the read path splits off, reaching v2 by minute 12)
9:40InterviewerHow do you get a feed for someone who follows 2,000 accounts?
9:50CandidateThat is the core deep dive. The naive query merges 2,000 timelines per request, which won't hit 500 ms at 100,000 reads per second. Let me finish the high-level picture in two minutes, then I'd like to spend real time on fan-out, including celebrities. OK?

What made this opening strong: the candidate proposed scope instead of asking open questions, turned each answer into a written requirement, derived the two biggest design decisions (precomputed feeds, media out of the database) from the numbers, and when the interviewer jumped ahead, acknowledged the question, placed it in the plan, and kept control of time.

How to practise

  • Time yourself. Run case studies against a 45-minute timer and note which phase overran. It is nearly always requirements or the high-level design.
  • Say it out loud. Practising silently trains a skill the interview does not test. Talk to a peer, or record yourself.
  • Learn the reference numbers. Latencies, throughputs and storage sizes from Estimation and Latency, Throughput & Availability make your reasoning fast and credible.
  • Collect trade-offs, not answers. For every component in this handbook, be able to say what it costs. "Consistent hashing moves 1/N of keys, but does nothing for hot keys" is worth more than knowing its name.
  • Read real designs. Engineering blog posts describe systems under real constraints, including the mistakes. They give you concrete examples to cite.
Interview angle

When to use it

In every design interview. Say the plan in one sentence at the start ("requirements, a quick estimate, API and data model, a high-level design, then deep dives on the hard parts") and then follow it visibly, so the interviewer can redirect you cheaply.

Follow-ups to expect

  • "What would you change if traffic grew 10 times?" Name the first bottleneck and the next architecture step, not a whole new design.
  • "Which part of this design are you least confident about?" Have an honest answer; it shows self-assessment.
  • "How would you roll this out?" Feature flags, shadow traffic, gradual migration, backfills.
  • "What would you monitor?" User-facing latency and error rate first, then saturation of the bottleneck you identified.
  • "Can you do this with fewer components?" Be ready to remove anything that is not pulling its weight.

Red flags

  • No requirements written down before the first box.
  • Choosing technologies by brand ("Kafka because it's what big companies use") rather than by property.
  • Never mentioning a trade-off, a failure, or a number.
  • Ignoring the interviewer's hints, or arguing past them.
  • Reaching minute 40 with no deep dive.
