A video platform like YouTube or Netflix accepts a multi-gigabyte upload over a shaky connection, turns it into a dozen versions for every screen and network, and then streams it to millions of viewers who expect playback to start in two seconds and never pause to buffer. Almost every byte goes out through caches near the viewer. The interesting parts are resumable uploads, a parallel transcoding pipeline, adaptive bitrate streaming, and a delivery network that can push hundreds of terabits per second.

How to use this case study

Sections follow the order of a real interview. Concepts used here have their own chapters: Blob & Object Storage, Content Delivery Networks, Message Queues, Batch vs Stream Processing.

1. Requirements

Functional

Upload videos up to 10 GB, resumable after a dropped connection. Process them into streamable formats with thumbnails. Watch on phones, browsers and TVs, with quality adapting to the network. Video pages with title, description and view count.

Non-functional

Playback starts in under 2 seconds and rarely rebuffers. Uploads are never lost once acknowledged. A new upload is watchable within minutes. Global audience, very high availability for playback, and cost efficiency, because bandwidth is the largest bill.

Out of scope

Recommendations, search, comments, live streaming, ads and DRM licence servers. Mention live streaming as a variant (same segments and manifests, but produced in real time).

The key observation: the system is two almost independent halves. Upload and processing is a batch pipeline that can take minutes and must never lose data. Playback is a read path that must be instant, and is served almost entirely by caches, so the job of the core system is mostly to prepare files and tell players where to find them.

2. Estimation

Use YouTube-scale published figures: about 500 hours of video uploaded per minute and around a billion hours watched per day. Assume uploads average an 8 Mbps 1080p source, views average 6 minutes, and the average delivered bitrate is 2.5 Mbps.

Three conclusions. Egress at 100+ Tbps cannot come from a few data centres; it has to be served from caches inside or next to viewers' ISPs. Storage grows by exabytes per year, so long-tail videos must move to cheaper storage tiers. And transcoding is a large, parallel batch workload that should run on preemptible capacity behind a queue.

The playback endpoints are not part of our API at all: the player fetches the manifest and segments from the CDN by URL. The metadata API only hands out a signed manifest URL with an expiry, and the same token is carried on every segment request (a signed cookie, a token in the path, or signatures rewritten into the segment URLs), so the CDN can reject hotlinking and expired links without calling back to us.

Video status is a small state machine: uploading → processing → ready, with failed and blocked as side exits. Every pipeline stage updates it, and the watch page shows "processing" until the first playable rendition exists.

5. High-level design

  1. Upload. The client splits the file into 8 MiB parts and uploads them, several in parallel, usually straight to the object store with presigned URLs. On complete, the upload service verifies the checksum, assembles the object and publishes upload_done.
  2. Process. The orchestrator builds a DAG of tasks for the video (probe, split, encode every chunk in every rendition, package, thumbnails, captions, safety checks) and feeds them to a worker pool. Outputs go to the origin store; the status becomes ready.
  3. Watch. The watch page calls the metadata API, which returns details and a signed manifest URL. The player fetches the manifest and then segments from the nearest CDN edge, switching renditions as bandwidth changes.
  4. Count. The player sends periodic watch events. A stream job filters bots and duplicates and updates the view count.

6. Deep dive: resumable chunked upload

A 2 GB upload over a phone connection takes many minutes, and the connection will drop. A single HTTP request would restart from zero every time. Instead the file is split into fixed-size parts that are uploaded and acknowledged independently.

  • Part size. 8 MiB balances retry cost (a lost part wastes at most 8 MiB) against request overhead. S3 multipart uploads require parts of at least 5 MiB (except the last) and allow up to 10,000 parts, which caps a file at about 78 GiB with 8 MiB parts; pick the part size from the file size.
  • Integrity. Each part carries a checksum the store verifies, and the final assemble step checks the whole-file hash the client declared at the start. A corrupted part is rejected and resent, never silently stored.
  • Direct to storage. Upload servers hand out presigned part URLs and never proxy the bytes, so 240 Gbps of ingest lands on the object store's front end, which is built for it. The open tus protocol standardises the same resumable pattern over plain HTTP.
  • Cleanup. Abandoned uploads leave orphaned parts that are billed like any other storage. A lifecycle rule aborts incomplete multipart uploads after a few days.

7. Deep dive: the transcoding pipeline

A source file is one codec at one resolution in a container no browser may support. Players need several resolutions and bitrates (the bitrate ladder), in codecs every device can decode, cut into short segments. Encoding a long video serially would take hours, so the work is split in two dimensions: by time chunk and by rendition.

  • Split at keyframes. Chunks must start on a keyframe (a closed GOP), or the encoder of chunk 2 would need frames from chunk 1. Encode each chunk independently, then concatenate. Splitting a 12-minute video into 60 chunks turns a 40-minute serial encode into well under a minute of wall-clock time on enough workers.
  • The ladder. A typical H.264 ladder runs from 240p at 0.4 Mbps to 1080p at 5 Mbps, plus 1440p and 4K for sources that have them. Netflix showed that a fixed ladder wastes bits: a cartoon needs far less bitrate than a football match at the same quality, so they choose the ladder per title, and later per shot, from measured quality scores.
  • Codecs. H.264 plays everywhere. VP9, HEVC and AV1 save roughly 30 to 50% bandwidth at the same quality, but cost far more CPU to encode and are not universal. Encode H.264 for every video, and the expensive codecs only for videos that get enough views to repay the encoding cost. A video with 12 views does not need AV1.
  • Packaging. Segments are written once as fragmented MP4 (CMAF) and described by both an HLS playlist (Apple devices) and a DASH manifest (most others), so one copy of the media serves both protocols.
  • Scheduling. Tasks go through a queue to a worker pool that scales on queue depth and runs largely on preemptible machines; a preempted task is simply retried. Priority lanes let a creator with millions of subscribers jump ahead of a backlog of bulk uploads.

Publish early: make the video watchable as soon as the low renditions are ready, and add higher ones as they finish. Viewers in the first minutes get 480p instead of a "processing" page.

8. Deep dive: adaptive bitrate streaming

With adaptive bitrate (ABR) streaming, the server does nothing clever. It serves plain files over HTTP. The player downloads a manifest that lists the renditions, then fetches 2 to 6 second segments one at a time, and before each request decides which rendition to take based on measured throughput and how many seconds of video it has buffered.

Because every rendition is cut at the same timestamps, the player can switch at any segment boundary without a glitch. The figure below is the exact output of the simple player logic after it: bandwidth is 9 Mbps for eight segments, then collapses to 1.2 Mbps.

LADDER = [(240, 0.4), (360, 0.8), (480, 1.4), (720, 2.8), (1080, 5.0)]   # (height, Mbps)
SEG = 4.0          # seconds of video per segment
MAX_BUFFER = 30.0  # stop fetching ahead beyond this


def pick(estimate_mbps, buffer_s):
    """Highest rendition that fits a safety share of measured throughput."""
    if estimate_mbps is None:
        return LADDER[2]                         # no measurement yet: start at 480p
    share = 0.8 if buffer_s >= 10 else 0.5       # thin buffer: be more careful
    fitting = [r for r in LADDER if r[1] <= share * estimate_mbps]
    return fitting[-1] if fitting else LADDER[0]


def play(network_mbps):
    buffer, estimate = 0.0, None
    for i, bw in enumerate(network_mbps, 1):
        height, rate = pick(estimate, buffer)
        download_s = rate * SEG / bw
        buffer = max(0.0, buffer - download_s)   # playback drains while downloading
        buffer = min(MAX_BUFFER, buffer + SEG)
        estimate = bw                            # real players smooth this (EWMA)
        print(f"seg {i:2}  {height:4}p  fetched in {download_s:4.1f} s  buffer {buffer:4.1f} s")


play([9.0] * 8 + [1.2] * 4)                      # bandwidth collapses at segment 9
# seg  1   480p  fetched in  0.6 s  buffer  4.0 s
# seg  5  1080p  fetched in  2.2 s  buffer 14.0 s
# seg  9  1080p  fetched in 16.7 s  buffer  6.7 s
# seg 10   240p  fetched in  1.3 s  buffer  9.4 s
# seg 12   360p  fetched in  2.7 s  buffer 13.4 s   (full output plotted above)

Real players are more refined. Throughput-based rules react fast but oscillate on noisy networks; buffer-based rules (Netflix's BBA, the BOLA algorithm used in dash.js) choose mostly from buffer level, which is stable and avoids rebuffering. Production players blend both, smooth the throughput estimate, and abandon a slow segment download mid-flight, which would have saved most of segment 9's 16.7 seconds here.

9. Deep dive: CDN delivery and edge caches

At 104 Tbps average, delivery is the system. Video is a near-perfect CDN workload: segments are immutable, so they can be cached forever with no invalidation, and popularity is extremely skewed, so a small fraction of the catalogue covers most watch time.

  • Embedded edge appliances. Netflix's Open Connect and Google's Global Cache place storage-heavy servers inside ISP networks and at internet exchanges. The ISP saves transit costs; the platform gets a cache a few milliseconds from the viewer.
  • Proactive fill. Netflix's catalogue is curated and predictable, so it predicts tomorrow's popular titles and copies them to appliances during the off-peak hours when links are idle. YouTube's catalogue is far larger and more volatile, so it relies more on pull-through caching with popularity-aware admission (do not let a one-off view evict a hot video).
  • Steering. A control plane in the cloud picks which appliances a player should use, based on the viewer's network, appliance health and what each appliance holds, and returns a ranked list of URLs. The player fails over to the next URL on errors.
  • Cache keys. Strip the signature query parameters from the cache key, or every viewer's signed URL becomes a separate cache entry. The edge validates the signature, then looks up the object by its path alone. See CDNs.

Counting views

116,000 view events per second is a classic streaming aggregation: players send watch events to a collector, which publishes them to Kafka partitioned by video_id. A stream job drops duplicates (same session), filters bot patterns, counts a view once playback passes a threshold such as 30 seconds, and adds per-video totals to a counter store every few seconds. Viral videos make one partition hot, so pre-aggregate in the collector before publishing. The public count is allowed to lag and is reconciled by a daily batch job over the raw events; that batch job is also where fraud filtering that needs more context runs.

11. Key trade-offs to state

  • Segmented HTTP streaming over a custom streaming server: any CDN can serve it and the server stays stateless, at the cost of a few seconds of latency, which only matters for live streaming.
  • Chunk-parallel encoding: minutes instead of hours to publish, at the cost of orchestration and slightly worse compression at chunk boundaries.
  • Codec choice by popularity: expensive codecs only where the bandwidth saving repays the encoding cost.
  • Short segments: 2 seconds adapt faster and start sooner; 6 seconds mean fewer requests and better compression. Four seconds is a common middle.
  • Push vs pull edge fill: push suits a predictable catalogue (Netflix), pull suits an unpredictable long tail (YouTube).
Interview angle

What the interviewer is checking

Whether you split the problem into an offline pipeline and an online read path, size the egress and conclude that CDNs carry the load, and can explain adaptive bitrate streaming mechanically rather than as a buzzword.

Follow-ups to expect

  • The upload fails at 80%. What does the client do?
  • How do you get a one-hour video watchable within a few minutes?
  • How does the player decide when to switch quality, and what does a switch look like on the wire?
  • How do you stop people hotlinking your segments from their own site?
  • Storage is growing by petabytes a day. What do you delete or move?
  • What changes for live streaming? (Segments produced in real time, a sliding-window playlist, shorter segments, lower latency targets.)

Red flags

  • Streaming video bytes through application servers.
  • Uploading a 10 GB file as one request with no resume.
  • Transcoding the whole file serially on one machine.
  • One rendition for all devices and networks.
  • Invalidating CDN caches for immutable segments.
