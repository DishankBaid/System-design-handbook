A web crawler downloads pages, extracts their links, and follows those links to download more, for a search engine, an archive or a training corpus. The loop fits on a whiteboard. Running it over a billion pages a month means deciding which of billions of known URLs to fetch next, never hammering a single website, recognising the same URL spelled ten ways and the same article served at a hundred URLs, escaping infinite link mazes, and revisiting pages roughly as often as they change.

How to use this case study

Sections follow the order of a real interview. Concepts used here have their own chapters: Probabilistic Data Structures (Bloom filters), Consistent Hashing, Message Queues, Blob Storage, DNS.

1. Requirements

Functional

Start from seed URLs and discover the web by following links. Download HTML pages and store them with metadata (fetch time, status, headers). Respect robots.txt. Recrawl pages to keep the collection fresh. Skip duplicate URLs and duplicate content.

Non-functional

1 billion pages per month. Polite: never more than about one request at a time per host, with a delay between requests. Robust against malformed HTML, slow servers, crawler traps and hostile sites. Horizontally scalable and restartable without losing the frontier.

Out of scope

Indexing and ranking, rendering JavaScript-heavy pages (mention a headless-browser tier for a small fraction of URLs), images and video.

The key observation: a crawler's throughput is limited by politeness, not by hardware. A single machine can open thousands of connections, but each website must be visited slowly. The design is mostly about choosing what to fetch and from where, so that the fetchers always have a host they are allowed to visit.

At 386 pages per second, raw throughput is modest: a few machines can do it. The numbers that shape the design are the last two. The crawler needs hundreds of distinct, polite-to-visit hosts ready at every instant, and the frontier must be organised by host so it can find them. The seen-URL set, at billions of entries, has to be compact.

Operators also need controls: a per-host override for crawl rate (some sites ask to be crawled slower, some partners faster), a blocklist, and a way to inject URLs at high priority, such as a sitemap that was just updated.

5. High-level design

  1. Select. A fetcher asks the frontier for work. The frontier returns a URL whose host is allowed to be fetched now, chosen by priority.
  2. Resolve and check. The fetcher resolves the host through a local caching DNS resolver and checks the cached robots.txt rules for the path. A host without fresh rules gets its robots.txt fetched first.
  3. Fetch. Download with a strict timeout (say 10 s), a size cap (say 10 MB), redirect limits and an honest user agent with a contact URL. Record status, headers and timing.
  4. Store and parse. Append the raw response to the content store. Parse HTML, compute the content fingerprint and SimHash, and skip link extraction for near-duplicates.
  5. Filter and enqueue. Normalise each extracted link, drop the ones that fail filters or are already seen, and add the rest to the frontier with a priority.

6. Deep dive: the URL frontier

The frontier answers one question, many hundreds of times per second: which URL next? A single FIFO queue fails immediately, because pages link heavily within their own site. Pop the first URL from en.wikipedia.org and the next thousand URLs are also from Wikipedia, so either the crawler hammers one host or the fetchers sit idle. The classic answer is the frontier from the Mercator crawler: two layers of queues, one for priority and one for politeness.

  • Front queues (priority). The prioritizer scores each URL: estimated importance (link-based rank of the page or its site), how stale the copy is, and whether it came from a sitemap. Higher scores go to higher queues, and the router draws from them with a bias, for example 50% from F1, 25% from F2, so low-priority work still progresses.
  • Back queues (politeness). Each back queue holds URLs of exactly one host, and there are a few times as many back queues as fetcher threads. When a back queue empties, the router pulls URLs from the front queues until it finds one for a host that has no back queue yet, and assigns it.
  • The heap. A min-heap keyed by each host's next allowed fetch time. A fetcher pops the earliest entry, sleeps until that time if needed, fetches one URL, and pushes the host back with a new time.

Billions of URLs do not fit in memory, so each queue is a file on local disk with only its head and tail buffered in RAM. Appends go to the tail buffer and are flushed in large sequential writes; reads refill the head buffer the same way. The frontier is checkpointed regularly, so a crashed node restarts from its last checkpoint and loses minutes of discovery, not the crawl.

robots.txt and DNS

  • robots.txt is fetched once per host and cached. RFC 9309 says crawlers should not use a cached copy for more than 24 hours, must parse at least 500 KiB of it, may treat a 4xx response as "no restrictions", and must treat a 5xx or unreachable robots.txt as "disallow everything" (or keep using a cached copy) until it can be fetched again. Match the most specific Allow/Disallow rule for the path, and honour Crawl-delay where given even though the RFC does not define it.
  • DNS is a hidden bottleneck: a standard resolver call is synchronous, can take hundreds of milliseconds, and public resolvers rate-limit heavy users. Run a local caching resolver on every crawler node, resolve asynchronously, and prefetch: when a URL enters a back queue, resolve its host before its turn comes.

7. Deep dive: deduplication

Duplicate URLs

The same page appears under many spellings: different case in the host, default ports, // in the path, reordered query parameters, tracking parameters, fragments. Normalise every URL before checking whether it has been seen, then check a Bloom filter of fingerprints. A Bloom filter can say "seen" for a URL that was not (a false positive, here 1%), which means that URL is never crawled while that filter lives (rotating or rebuilding the filter per crawl cycle gives it another chance); it can never say "not seen" for a seen URL, so it never causes a duplicate fetch. For important URLs, confirm a "seen" answer against the exact on-disk set.

Duplicate content

Different URLs often serve the same content: mirrors, syndicated articles, printer-friendly versions, session-id variants that slipped past normalisation. An exact hash of the body catches byte-identical copies. Most copies are not byte-identical, though: they differ in an ad, a timestamp or a footer. SimHash gives similar documents fingerprints that differ in only a few bits.

import hashlib
import re
from urllib.parse import parse_qsl, urlencode, urlsplit, urlunsplit

TRACKING = {"utm_source", "utm_medium", "utm_campaign", "gclid", "fbclid", "sessionid"}


def normalize(url):
    """One spelling per page, so the seen-set catches duplicates."""
    s = urlsplit(url.strip())
    scheme, host = s.scheme.lower(), (s.hostname or "").lower()
    port = s.port
    netloc = host if port in (None, 80 if scheme == "http" else 443) else f"{host}:{port}"
    path = re.sub(r"/{2,}", "/", s.path) or "/"
    query = urlencode(sorted((k, v) for k, v in parse_qsl(s.query) if k.lower() not in TRACKING))
    return urlunsplit((scheme, netloc, path, query, ""))          # fragment dropped


def simhash(text, bits=64):
    """Charikar's SimHash over 3-word shingles: similar texts get close fingerprints."""
    words = re.findall(r"\w+", text.lower())
    weights = [0] * bits
    for i in range(len(words) - 2):
        h = int.from_bytes(hashlib.md5(" ".join(words[i:i + 3]).encode()).digest()[:8], "big")
        for b in range(bits):
            weights[b] += 1 if h >> b & 1 else -1
    return sum(1 << b for b in range(bits) if weights[b] > 0)


def distance(a, b):
    return bin(a ^ b).count("1")


print(normalize("HTTPS://News.Example.com:443//world/eu-summit?utm_source=x&b=2&a=1#comments"))
# https://news.example.com/world/eu-summit?a=1&b=2

article = ("European leaders met in Brussels on Thursday to agree a common plan for energy prices "
           "after a month of negotiations. The summit ended with a joint statement on gas storage, "
           "grid investment and support for households facing higher bills this winter. Ministers "
           "will meet again in November to agree how the cost is shared between member states, and "
           "the commission will publish draft rules for joint gas purchasing before the end of the "
           "month. Several countries asked for a cap on wholesale prices, which was left out of the "
           "final text after objections from the largest importers. Analysts said the agreement was "
           "less ambitious than expected but would calm markets ahead of the heating season. ")
mirror = article.replace("on Thursday", "on Thursday evening") + "Updated 10:41 CET."
other = ("The city council approved a new cycling network on Monday, adding forty kilometres of "
         "protected lanes over three years and closing two central streets to through traffic. "
         "Shops on the affected streets will get delivery windows each morning, and bus routes "
         "will move to parallel avenues. Opponents said the plan was rushed, while cycling groups "
         "called it the biggest change to the city centre in a generation. ")
a, b, c = simhash(article), simhash(mirror), simhash(other)
print(f"{a:016x} {b:016x} {c:016x}")
# 9c906dfd5cbaa229 1c906dfd1cbaa629 b1893933b0516fc5
print("article vs mirror:", distance(a, b), "bits   article vs other:", distance(a, c), "bits")
# article vs mirror: 3 bits   article vs other: 36 bits

Finding whether any of billions of stored fingerprints is within 3 bits of a new one cannot be a linear scan. Google's crawler paper splits each 64-bit fingerprint into blocks and keeps several sorted tables, each keyed by a different subset of blocks: by the pigeonhole principle, two fingerprints that differ in at most 3 bits must agree exactly on at least one of 4 blocks, so each table lookup is an exact-match range query on that block. Near-duplicate pages are stored (cheaply, as a pointer to the original) but their links are not re-extracted, which also kills a whole class of crawler traps.

Crawler traps

Some sites generate infinite URL spaces, by accident or on purpose: a calendar with a "next month" link forever, faceted search with every filter combination, session ids in paths, symbolic-link loops such as /a/b/a/b/a/b/, and soft-404 pages that return 200 for any path. Defences are budgets and heuristics, applied in the URL filter:

  • A maximum URL length (2,048 characters) and path depth.
  • A per-host page budget per crawl cycle, scaled by the host's importance, so a trap can waste at most its host's budget.
  • Rejecting paths with repeating segments, and query strings with more than a handful of parameters.
  • Detecting soft 404s by fetching a random nonsense path once per host and comparing SimHashes with suspicious pages.

8. Deep dive: recrawling and distribution

Recrawl scheduling by change rate

Pages change at wildly different rates: a news homepage every few minutes, an article rarely after its first day, a company's About page once a year. Recrawling everything every month wastes most of the budget on unchanged pages and still misses the fast ones. The scheduler keeps, per URL, a history of whether each visit found a change (compare the content hash), estimates a change rate from it, and sets the next visit accordingly:

  • A simple adaptive rule: if the page changed since the last visit, halve the interval; if not, multiply it by 1.5, bounded between, say, 1 hour and 60 days.
  • Weight by importance: an important page that changes daily is worth visiting daily; an obscure one that changes daily may only be worth weekly.
  • Use cheap signals first: conditional requests with If-Modified-Since or If-None-Match return 304 Not Modified without a body, and sitemaps list lastmod dates.

Cho and Garcia-Molina showed a counter-intuitive result here: with a fixed crawl budget, visiting the very fastest-changing pages as often as possible can lower average freshness, because they go stale again almost immediately. Spending on pages that change at moderate rates keeps more of the collection fresh.

Partitioning the crawl by host

Partition by host, not by URL. If URLs of one host were spread over many nodes, each node would enforce its own delay and the host would get several times the intended rate; robots.txt would be fetched by every node; and the seen-set would need a network call per link. With host ownership, a node that extracts a link to a host it does not own appends it to an outbound batch for the owner, flushed every second or every few thousand URLs. Assign hosts to nodes with consistent hashing, so adding a node moves only a fraction of hosts, together with their frontier queues and seen-set shards.

One refinement: a handful of giant hosts (Wikipedia, large forums) have far more URLs than average. They are still fetched politely, one request at a time, so their URLs simply wait longer in their back queue; the per-host budget keeps them from crowding out everything else.

10. Key trade-offs to state

  • Politeness over speed: one request per host at a time and adaptive delays cap the crawl rate per site; parallelism comes from many hosts, not many connections to one.
  • Bloom filter over an exact set: 12 GB instead of 80 GB for 10 billion URLs, in exchange for never discovering about 1% of genuinely new URLs unless the filter is rebuilt.
  • Host-partitioned nodes: local politeness and dedup, in exchange for shipping links between nodes and uneven host sizes.
  • Adaptive recrawl over fixed cycles: fresher collection for the same budget, in exchange for per-URL history and scheduling logic.
  • Near-duplicate suppression: saves storage and stops many traps, at the risk of occasionally treating two genuinely different pages as copies.
Interview angle

What the interviewer is checking

Whether you go past "BFS with a queue" to a frontier that enforces politeness and priority, whether you handle URL and content duplicates with appropriate data structures, and whether you know the practical hazards: robots.txt, DNS, traps and freshness.

Follow-ups to expect

  • How do you make sure you never send more than one request per second to a site?
  • How do you detect that two different URLs are the same page? What about the same article on two sites?
  • Your crawler is stuck in a calendar that links to the next month forever. How do you notice and stop?
  • How do you split the work across 50 machines without breaking politeness?
  • How often should you recrawl a page?
  • How would you crawl pages that need JavaScript to render?

Red flags

  • A single global FIFO queue.
  • Partitioning by URL hash, so one host is hit by every node.
  • An exact in-memory hash set for billions of URLs with no size estimate.
  • Ignoring robots.txt or treating it as optional.
  • No defence against infinite URL spaces.
