Why it matters

"Is the system fast and up?" sounds like two yes-or-no questions. In practice both are distributions. A checkout API with an average latency of 50 ms can still make one customer in a hundred wait half a second, and a service that is "up" can fail 1% of requests for a whole afternoon. Interviewers want to hear you talk about these qualities in numbers: percentiles, utilisation, nines and error budgets.

This chapter defines the vocabulary precisely, then covers the four mechanisms that matter most in design discussions: Little's law, tail latency and fan-out, queueing near full utilisation, and how availability composes across components. It ends with how teams turn all of this into targets they can operate against.

Latency, throughput and bandwidth

Latency

The time one operation takes, from the moment it is issued until the result arrives. Usually measured in milliseconds at the client or the server. A distribution, never a single number.

Throughput

How many operations complete per unit of time: requests per second, messages per second, MB per second. A rate.

Bandwidth

The maximum throughput a channel can carry, such as a 10 Gbps link. Throughput is what you actually achieve.

Service time vs response time

Service time is the work itself. Response time is service time plus time spent waiting in queues. Under load, waiting usually dominates.

Latency and throughput are related but not opposites. Batching raises throughput and also raises latency, because items wait for the batch to fill. Adding parallel servers raises throughput without changing the latency of one request. And a system near its throughput limit has terrible latency, which the queueing section explains. A useful image is a highway: latency is how long one trip takes, throughput is how many cars pass per hour, bandwidth is the number of lanes.

Little's law

Little's law says that in any stable system, the average number of items inside equals the arrival rate times the average time each item spends inside:

L = λ × W L average number in the system (in-flight requests, queue length, open connections) λ average arrival rate (requests per second) W average time in the system (seconds)

It needs no assumptions about distributions, which makes it the most useful formula in capacity planning. Three typical uses:

In flight 2,000 req/s × 0.050 s = 100 concurrent requests DB pool 2,000 req/s × 0.010 s holding a connection = 20 connections busy on average Max rate pool of 50 connections ÷ 0.010 s each = 5,000 req/s before requests queue

The third line runs the law backwards: a fixed concurrency limit divided by latency caps throughput. That is why a slow downstream dependency reduces throughput even when CPU is idle. If database calls slow from 10 ms to 100 ms, the same 50-connection pool now supports only 500 requests per second, and everything beyond that waits. Thread pools, connection pools and in-flight request limits all behave this way.

Percentiles and tail latency

Latency distributions are skewed: most requests are fast, and a long tail is slow because of garbage-collection pauses, cache misses, lock waits, retries and noisy neighbours. The mean hides this. A percentile answers the question users care about: "how slow is it for the unlucky ones?" The p99 is the latency that 99% of requests beat and 1% exceed.

  • p50 (median) describes the typical request. Good for spotting regressions in the common path.
  • p95 and p99 describe the experience of the unlucky. For a user making 100 requests during a session, hitting the p99 at least once is more likely than not.
  • p99.9 and max matter for high-volume or high-value paths, and for systems where one slow call blocks a whole batch.
Common mistake

Averaging percentiles. The p99 of a fleet is not the average of each server's p99, and you cannot compute a daily p99 from 1,440 per-minute p99 values. Percentiles must be computed from the underlying data or from mergeable histograms (HdrHistogram, t-digest, Prometheus histograms). Also measure latency at the client when you can: a server-side timer misses time spent waiting in its own accept queue.

The tail at scale: why p99 dominates fan-out

Many requests fan out: a search query hits 100 index shards, a page renders from 30 microservice calls, a timeline is hydrated from 20 cache lookups. The request is only as fast as its slowest call. If each call independently has probability p of being slow, the chance that at least one of N calls is slow is

P(at least one slow) = 1 - (1 - p)^N p = 1% N = 1 → 1.0% p = 1% N = 10 → 9.6% p = 1% N = 100 → 63.4%

This is the central point of Dean and Barroso's "The Tail at Scale": in large fan-out systems, rare per-server hiccups become the common case for users, and you cannot fix them by making servers faster on average. Techniques that work:

  • Hedged requests. Send the request to one replica; if no answer arrives within, say, the p95, send it to a second replica and use whichever answers first. Costs a few percent extra load and cuts the tail dramatically.
  • Tied requests. Enqueue on two servers at once, and let the first to start processing cancel the other.
  • Reduce fan-out. Fewer, larger shards; batch lookups; precompute.
  • Return partial results when some shards miss the deadline, if the product allows it (search results usually can).
  • Set deadlines and propagate them, so work for an already-abandoned request stops. See Timeouts, Retries & Circuit Breakers.

Queueing: why latency explodes near 100% utilisation

A server busy 50% of the time often finds a request waiting when it finishes another; at 90% it almost always does, and the queue keeps growing during every burst. The simplest model, the M/M/1 queue (random arrivals, random service times, one server), gives a closed form for the mean response time:

W = S / (1 - ρ) S mean service time (the work itself) ρ utilisation = arrival rate / service rate (must be below 1) ρ = 50% → W = 2 S ρ = 90% → W = 10 S ρ = 80% → W = 5 S ρ = 95% → W = 20 S

Three practical consequences:

  • Do not plan for 100%. Target 50% to 70% steady-state utilisation on latency-sensitive tiers. The "wasted" capacity is what keeps the queue short during bursts.
  • The tail is worse than the mean. In M/M/1, response time is exponentially distributed, so the p99 is about 4.6 times the mean: at 90% utilisation the p99 is about 46 times the bare service time.
  • Small load changes cause large latency changes near the knee. A 5% traffic increase at 90% utilisation adds far more latency than the same increase at 50%, which is why latency alerts often fire long before CPU alerts. Queues that grow without bound are why systems need load shedding and backpressure.

Availability and the nines

Availability is the fraction of time, or of requests, for which the system works correctly. Modern teams usually measure it per request (good requests / total requests) rather than per minute of uptime, because a partial outage that fails 10% of requests is invisible to a ping check but very visible to users. It is quoted in "nines":

Each extra nine is ten times harder and usually much more than ten times as expensive. At four nines you have 4.3 minutes a month: no human can be paged, log in and fix anything in that time, so recovery must be automatic. A design claiming five nines should explain how every failure, including deploys and configuration changes, is handled without an operator.

Composing availability: series and parallel

When a request needs every component in a chain, the components are in series and their availabilities multiply. When any one of several redundant components can serve the request, they are in parallel and their unavailabilities multiply.

Series A = A₁ × A₂ × ... × Aₙ (never above the weakest part) Parallel A = 1 - (1 - A₁)(1 - A₂)...(1 - Aₙ) (never below the strongest part)

The parallel formula assumes two things that real systems often violate, and an interviewer may probe both:

  • Independent failures. Three replicas in one rack, behind one switch, running one bad config push, fail together. Redundancy only counts across independent failure domains: zones, regions, deploy waves.
  • Instant, correct failover. Promoting a database standby takes seconds to minutes, and failover logic is itself a component that can fail. Many large outages are failover systems misbehaving.

The same arithmetic gives a design rule for dependencies: a service that calls 10 others in series, each 99.9% available, cannot exceed 99.0% unless it tolerates some of them failing (with caching, defaults or graceful degradation).

Computing it

Both formulas and the fan-out probability in a few lines. The output in the comments is from a real run.

from math import prod

MIN_PER_YEAR = 365 * 24 * 60


def series(*parts):
    """All parts must be up: availabilities multiply."""
    return prod(parts)


def parallel(*replicas):
    """Up if at least one replica is up (independent failures, instant failover)."""
    return 1 - prod(1 - a for a in replicas)


def p_any_slow(p_slow, fanout):
    """Probability that at least one of `fanout` independent calls is slow."""
    return 1 - (1 - p_slow) ** fanout


def show(name, a):
    print(f"{name:28s} {a:.6%}  ~{(1 - a) * MIN_PER_YEAR:,.0f} min down/year")


show("LB -> app -> db", series(0.9999, 0.999, 0.9995))
show("LB -> 3 apps -> db pair", series(0.9999, parallel(0.995, 0.995, 0.995), parallel(0.999, 0.999)))
for n in (1, 10, 100):
    print(f"fan-out {n:3d}: P(request sees a p99 tail) = {p_any_slow(0.01, n):.1%}")

# LB -> 3 apps -> db pair      99.989888%  ~53 min down/year
# fan-out   1: P(request sees a p99 tail) = 1.0%
# fan-out  10: P(request sees a p99 tail) = 9.6%
# fan-out 100: P(request sees a p99 tail) = 63.4%

SLIs, SLOs, SLAs and error budgets

Google's SRE practice gave the industry a precise vocabulary for targets:

SLI (indicator)

A measured ratio of good events to total events. "Fraction of GET /feed requests that return 2xx in under 300 ms, measured at the load balancer."

SLO (objective)

An internal target for an SLI over a window. "99.9% of feed requests are good, over a rolling 28 days." Owned by engineering.

SLA (agreement)

A contract with customers, with consequences (usually service credits) when missed. Should always be looser than the SLO, so you breach your own target before you owe anyone money.

Error budget

One minus the SLO: the amount of failure you are allowed. A 99.9% SLO over 30 days at 100 million requests is a budget of 100,000 failed requests, or 43.2 minutes of full outage.

The error budget turns reliability into a resource to spend. While budget remains, teams ship features and take risks. When it runs out, the agreed policy kicks in: freeze risky launches, prioritise reliability work. This ends the usual argument between "ship faster" and "stay stable" with a number both sides accepted in advance. It also implies that 100% is the wrong target: users cannot tell 99.999% from 100% through their own Wi-Fi and phone networks, and the last nines cost the most.

  • Choose SLIs users would recognise: success rate and latency of user-facing operations, not CPU or disk usage.
  • Use latency percentiles in SLOs: "99% of requests under 300 ms" is meaningful; "mean under 100 ms" is not.
  • Alert on burn rate, meaning how fast the budget is being consumed, rather than on each error spike. Burning a month's budget in an hour pages someone; slow burns become tickets. See Observability.

Worked example: setting targets for a checkout API

A checkout service handles 300 requests per second at peak. Each request calls inventory, pricing and payment in series and writes an order to the database. The product requirement is "checkout must feel instant and almost never fail".

Interview angle

When to bring it up

During requirements, turn "fast and reliable" into numbers: "p99 under 200 ms for reads, 99.99% availability for the redirect path". In the design, use Little's law to size pools, the fan-out formula when a request touches many shards, and the series/parallel formulas when you add redundancy.

Follow-ups to expect

  • "Why not track average latency?" It hides the tail, and the tail is what fan-out amplifies.
  • "Your request touches 50 shards. What is your p99?" Use 1 - (1 - p)^N, then propose hedging or less fan-out.
  • "Can we run the servers at 90% CPU to save money?" Explain the queueing curve and what happens to p99.
  • "What availability can this design achieve?" Multiply the series chain; show the weakest component.
  • "What is an error budget and how would you use it?"

Red flags

  • Promising five nines with a single-region, single-database design.
  • Adding redundancy and assuming failures are independent when replicas share a rack, a config or a deploy.
  • Confusing throughput with latency, or bits with bytes when talking about bandwidth.
  • Averaging percentiles across servers or time windows.
