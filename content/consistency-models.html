Why it matters

A user changes their profile photo, the app says "saved", they refresh, and the old photo is back. Nothing was lost: the write went to the primary, and the refresh read from a replica that had not caught up. Whether that is a bug depends on which consistency model the system promises, meaning the rules about which values a read is allowed to return.

Replication, caching and multi-region deployment all create several copies of the same data, and copies drift. A consistency model is the contract between the storage system and its clients about how much drift clients can observe. Stronger models hide more of it and cost more latency and availability; weaker models are cheaper and push the complexity into the application. Knowing the ladder of models, and the mechanism behind each rung, lets you choose the cheapest model that still makes the product behave correctly.

This chapter covers single-object consistency models: what reads of replicated data can see. CAP & PACELC explains why the strongest model costs availability during partitions; Replication covers the machinery that creates the copies.

Consistency vs isolation

Two different questions are often both called "consistency":

Consistency models

About replicas and recency: when one client writes a value, which values can other clients read, and in what order do they see updates? Usually defined for single operations on single objects.

Isolation levels

About transactions and interleaving: when several multi-object transactions run concurrently, which anomalies (lost updates, write skew) can occur? Serializability is the strongest. See ACID & Isolation Levels.

Strict serializability

Both at once: transactions behave as if executed one at a time, and that order respects real time, so a transaction that commits before another starts is ordered first. Spanner calls this external consistency.

The axes are independent. A serializable database can return stale results if a read-only transaction is served from a lagging replica (serializability allows ordering it "in the past"). A linearizable key-value store says nothing about transactions spanning two keys. Strict serializability is the combination of serializability and linearizability.

The hierarchy

Models form a partial order: a stronger model allows fewer histories, so every history it allows is also allowed by the weaker models below it.

The models

Linearizability

Linearizability, defined by Herlihy and Wing in 1990, is the strongest single-object model. Every operation appears to take effect atomically at some instant between its invocation and its response, and the order of these instants respects real time. Practically: once a write is acknowledged, every read that starts afterwards, from any client, sees it or a later value. The system behaves as if there were one copy of the data.

It is what you need for anything that relies on uniqueness or a single source of truth at a point in time: leader election, distributed locks, "username must be unique", and compare-and-set on an account balance. It is the C in CAP.

Sequential consistency

Sequential consistency (Lamport, 1979) requires that all operations appear in some single total order, and that each client's operations appear in that order in the sequence that client issued them. Unlike linearizability, the order need not match real time: a read may return an old value as long as everyone agrees on one global sequence. It is common in discussions of CPU memory models and rare as a database guarantee, but it is a useful step to understand what "real time" adds.

Causal consistency

Two operations are causally related if one could have influenced the other: the same client did one after the other, or one read a value the other wrote, or a chain of such links connects them. Lamport's 1978 "happened-before" relation formalises this. Causal consistency requires that every client sees causally related writes in causal order. Concurrent writes, with no causal link, may be seen in different orders by different clients.

The classic example: Alice posts "I lost my job", then, after reading supportive replies, posts "Never mind, got a new one!". Bob replies "Congratulations" to the second post. Under causal consistency nobody can see Bob's congratulations without the post it answers. Mahajan, Alvisi and Dahlin showed in 2011 that a variant of causal consistency is the strongest model a system can provide while staying available during partitions, which makes it attractive for geo-replicated systems.

Session guarantees

Terry and colleagues defined four guarantees in 1994 for a single client session. They are much cheaper than global models, because they only constrain what one client sees, and they fix the most visible anomalies:

GuaranteePromiseAnomaly it prevents
Read-your-writesA client's reads reflect all its own earlier writes"I saved my profile photo, refreshed, and the old one came back"
Monotonic readsOnce a client has seen a value, later reads never return an older oneA comment appears, then disappears on refresh, then reappears
Monotonic writesA client's writes are applied everywhere in the order it issued them"Set price to $10" then "set price to $12" ends as $10 on some replica
Writes-follow-readsA write that follows a read is ordered after the write that was readA reply stored on a replica that has not yet received the post it replies to

Causal consistency implies all four. Many products need little more than read-your-writes and monotonic reads; delivering just those two is often the right engineering answer.

Eventual consistency

Eventual consistency promises only that if writes stop, all replicas eventually converge to the same value. It says nothing about what you read in the meantime: stale values, values going backwards, and effects before causes are all allowed. It is a liveness property (something good eventually happens) with no safety property (nothing bad happens). It is also how most caches, DNS, CDNs and asynchronously replicated databases behave by default. Systems often strengthen it to strong eventual consistency, where replicas that have received the same updates are in the same state, which CRDTs provide.

Reading histories: allowed or forbidden?

A history is a record of operations, each with a start and end time, as clients observed them. Models are defined by which histories they allow. Each scenario below shows clients P1 to P4 on a real-time axis, with bars for the duration of each operation, and which models allow it.

Common mistake

Assuming that quorums (R + W > N) give linearizability. Overlapping quorums guarantee that a read contacts a replica with the latest acknowledged write, but with concurrent writes, sloppy quorums, or a write that failed partway, two successive reads can still return new then old values. Dynamo-style stores need extra steps, such as synchronous read repair, or a consensus protocol like Cassandra's lightweight transactions, to be linearizable.

Vector clocks: detecting concurrency

Causal consistency and conflict detection both need to answer one question about two versions: did one happen before the other, or are they concurrent? Physical timestamps cannot answer it, because clocks drift and because "earlier" in wall-clock time does not mean "seen by". A vector clock can. Each replica keeps a counter per replica. It increments its own entry on every write, and on receiving a version it takes the element-wise maximum. Version a happened before b if every entry of a is at most the matching entry of b and they differ; if neither precedes the other, they are concurrent and must be merged.

def happened_before(a, b):
    """True if the event with clock a causally precedes the event with clock b."""
    keys = a.keys() | b.keys()
    return a != b and all(a.get(k, 0) <= b.get(k, 0) for k in keys)


def compare(a, b):
    if happened_before(a, b):
        return "before"
    if happened_before(b, a):
        return "after"
    return "equal" if a == b else "concurrent"


class Replica:
    def __init__(self, name):
        self.name, self.clock = name, {}

    def local_write(self):
        self.clock[self.name] = self.clock.get(self.name, 0) + 1
        return dict(self.clock)          # the version stamped on this write

    def receive(self, remote_clock):
        for k, v in remote_clock.items():   # element-wise max, then count the receive
            self.clock[k] = max(self.clock.get(k, 0), v)
        return self.local_write()


a, b = Replica("A"), Replica("B")
v1 = a.local_write()       # cart = {book}           on A
v2 = b.receive(v1)         # B sees v1, adds a lamp
v3 = a.local_write()       # A adds a pen without having seen v2

print("v1", v1, "v2", v2, "v3", v3)
print("v1 vs v2:", compare(v1, v2))
print("v1 vs v3:", compare(v1, v3))
print("v2 vs v3:", compare(v2, v3))

# v1 {'A': 1} v2 {'A': 1, 'B': 1} v3 {'A': 2}
# v1 vs v2: before
# v1 vs v3: before
# v2 vs v3: concurrent

Both v2 and v3 descend from v1, so either may safely overwrite v1. But v2 and v3 are concurrent: a store using last-writer-wins would silently drop the lamp or the pen. A store using vector clocks keeps both as siblings and lets the application merge them (here, a set union gives {book, lamp, pen}). Dynamo and Riak use this approach. Vector clocks grow with the number of writers, so practical systems bound them: Dynamo truncated old entries, Riak moved to dotted version vectors keyed by replica rather than by client.

The cost of strong consistency

Every model above causal requires coordination on the operation path (for sequential consistency, either reads or writes can be local, but not both), and the price is set by the distance to the replicas that must agree:

Replica placementQuorum round trip per writeEffect
One zone~0.5 msNegligible next to disk fsync
Three zones in one region~1 to 2 msThe normal choice for linearizable stores such as etcd
Three regions on one continent~10 to 70 msNoticeable; fine for low-rate writes
Three regions on different continents~70 to 150 msEvery write waits for the nearest other region; cross-continent sequential writes become slow

Linearizable reads usually cost too: the leader must confirm it is still the leader, either with a quorum round trip or with a time-based lease that assumes bounded clock drift. Weaker models avoid this entirely by answering from the local replica. That gap of tens of milliseconds per operation, multiplied by every operation on a request path, is why so many systems choose causal or session guarantees for most data and reserve linearizability for the few operations that need it. This per-request trade-off is PACELC's "else" branch.

Choosing a model: a worked example

A social app has profiles, posts, comments, like counts and usernames. Pick the cheapest model per data type that keeps the product correct:

DataWhat must not happenModelMechanism
Username registrationTwo users with the same nameLinearizableConditional write on a consensus-backed store
Own profile editsSeeing your old photo after savingRead-your-writesRoute the editor's reads to the leader for 10 seconds after a write
Comment threadsA reply visible before the comment it answersCausalDependency tracking per write
Feed scrollingPosts disappearing on refreshMonotonic readsSticky replica per user session
Like countsNothing serious: a count off by a few for secondsEventualCRDT counter or async aggregation

Only one row needs the expensive model. Saying this out loud, per data type, is the strongest answer to "what consistency does your design need?"

In the real world

  • Google Spanner provides external consistency (strict serializability) across the globe, using TrueTime's bounded clock uncertainty to commit-wait out the ambiguity, typically a few milliseconds.
  • Amazon DynamoDB defaults to eventually consistent reads and offers strongly consistent reads per request within a region, at twice the read capacity cost.
  • Azure Cosmos DB exposes five named levels: strong, bounded staleness, session, consistent prefix and eventual. Session (read-your-writes and monotonic reads within a session token) is the default.
  • MongoDB offers causally consistent client sessions since version 3.6, and linearizable reads through a read concern on the primary.
  • Cassandra lets each request choose a consistency level (ONE, QUORUM, ALL and local variants) and uses Paxos-based lightweight transactions when linearizable compare-and-set is needed.
  • Jepsen analyses have found many databases violating their documented consistency claims under partitions and clock faults, a reminder to test the guarantee rather than trust the label.
Interview angle

When to bring it up

Whenever you add replicas, caches or regions. Name the model per data type, as in the worked example, and the mechanism that provides it. In particular, call out read-your-writes for any flow where a user edits something and then views it.

Follow-ups to expect

  • "A user updates their profile and sees the old version. Why, and how do you fix it?" Replica lag; read-your-writes via leader reads or a last-write token.
  • "What is the difference between linearizable and serializable?" Single object and real time versus transactions and any order; strict serializability is both.
  • "Does a quorum give strong consistency?" Not by itself; explain why.
  • "How do you detect concurrent writes?" Version vectors, and what to do with siblings.
  • "What does linearizability cost across regions?" A cross-region round trip per write, with numbers.

Red flags

  • Saying "strong consistency" without defining it, or using it for everything.
  • Using wall-clock timestamps to decide which concurrent write wins without acknowledging lost writes and clock skew.
  • Confusing consistency models with ACID consistency or isolation levels.
  • Treating "eventually consistent" as "consistent after a short delay" without saying what users see in the meantime.
