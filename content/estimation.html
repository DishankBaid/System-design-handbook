Why it matters

"Design a chat system for 500 million users" is not a design problem until you know what 500 million users means in requests per second, terabytes per day and machines. Is it one database or a thousand? Does the hot data fit in RAM? Will the network card saturate before the CPU does? Two minutes of arithmetic answers these questions, and the answers decide the architecture.

Back-of-envelope estimation is the habit of computing order-of-magnitude numbers quickly, with round inputs and stated assumptions. The goal is not precision. Being within a factor of two or three is plenty, because the decisions it drives are coarse: does this fit on one machine or need sharding, is this read path served from memory or disk, is the bandwidth bill a rounding error or the main cost.

In an interview the estimate sits right after requirements (see Approaching a Design Interview). It is short, and every number should end in a conclusion: "about 70,000 reads per second at peak, so the read path has to be served from cache".

The toolkit

Powers of two and data sizes

Storage and memory are counted in powers of two, network and disk vendors in powers of ten. The difference is under 13% even at the petabyte level, so in an estimate treat them as equal: 210 ≈ 103.

Useful sizes for individual values:

Identifiers

Integer id: 8 bytes. UUID: 16 bytes binary, 36 as text. Timestamp: 8 bytes. An IPv4 address: 4 bytes.

Text

ASCII character: 1 byte. UTF-8: 1 to 4 bytes per character. A 280-character post with author, ids and timestamps: about 300 bytes to 1 KB.

Media

Thumbnail: 10 to 50 KB. Phone photo: 1 to 4 MB. One minute of 1080p video at 5 Mbps: about 40 MB.

Overhead

Indexes, row headers and free space typically add 30% to 100% on top of raw values in a database. Replication multiplies everything by the replica count.

Latency numbers every programmer should know

Jeff Dean popularised this list at Google, building on a table in Peter Norvig's essay "Teach Yourself Programming in Ten Years". The exact values date from the late 2000s and some have moved since, but the ratios between tiers are what matter for design, and those have stayed remarkably stable: a memory reference is thousands of times faster than a datacenter round trip, which in turn is hundreds of times faster than crossing an ocean.

The lessons that survive every hardware generation:

  • Memory beats network beats disk seek. Reading a value from a cache in the same datacenter (one round trip, about 0.5 ms) is roughly 20 times faster than a random read on a spinning disk. That is the whole case for caching.
  • Sequential beats random. An HDD streams 1 MB in about the time of two seeks. Log-structured storage engines and Kafka exist because of this ratio (see Indexing).
  • Distance is physics. Light in fibre covers about 200 km per millisecond, so a round trip costs about 1 ms per 100 km of route. No upgrade fixes a 150 ms intercontinental round trip; only moving data closer does (see CDNs).
  • Count round trips, not instructions. A request that makes 10 sequential calls to a database in another region spends roughly a second waiting, whatever the CPU does.

The seconds-per-day trick

Almost every estimate converts "per day" into "per second". A day has 86,400 seconds. Round it to 105 and division becomes subtracting exponents: 109 requests a day is 104 per second. The shortcut underestimates by about 14%, which is noise next to your other assumptions; mention it and move on.

1 day = 86,400 s ≈ 10^5 s 1 month ≈ 2.6 million s ≈ 2.5 × 10^6 s 1 year ≈ 31.5 million s ≈ 3 × 10^7 s (π × 10^7 is a good mnemonic) 1 million / day ≈ 12 / s 1 billion / day ≈ 12,000 / s 1 request / s ≈ 86,400 / day ≈ 2.6 million / month

The method

Every estimate in an interview is a chain of a few multiplications. Write the chain down so the interviewer can follow it and correct one input without redoing everything.

Requests per second

Start from daily active users (DAU) and how often each does the action: QPS = DAU × actions per user per day ÷ 86,400. Compute reads and writes separately; their ratio is often the most important single number in the design. A 100:1 read-heavy system wants caches and precomputation. A 1:1 or write-heavy system (metrics, chat, logging) wants append-only storage and partitioning.

Peak factors

Traffic is not flat. Consumer products follow the sun, with a daily peak about 2 to 3 times the average. Events push harder: a sports final, a product launch or New Year's midnight can produce 5 to 10 times normal load for minutes. Size the system for peak, not average, and say which multiplier you used. When in doubt, 2× to 3× for daily peak is the conventional choice, with a note that known events need more.

Storage

storage = new items per day × bytes per item × days retained × replication factor, plus index and format overhead. Two things dominate in practice. First, media dwarfs metadata: a 2 MB photo weighs as much as several thousand text posts, so estimate them separately and expect different stores. Second, growth dominates the multi-year number. A five-year storage figure computed with today's upload rate can be off by more than two times.

Bandwidth

bandwidth = QPS × bytes per request or response. Compute ingress (uploads) and egress (downloads) separately; for media products egress is usually far larger and is the line item that justifies a CDN. Convert to bits when comparing with network links: 1 GB/s is 8 Gbps. A single server typically has a 10 to 100 Gbps NIC, but serving at more than half of line rate for long is optimistic.

Memory for caches

Access patterns are usually skewed: a small fraction of items gets most of the reads. A common rule of thumb is to cache the hottest 20% of a day's read volume, then check that against server RAM. Ask: does the hot set fit on one machine (under a few hundred GB), on a small cluster (a few TB), or not at all? The answer decides between an in-process cache, a Redis or Memcached cluster, or redesigning what you cache. Store compact representations: a list of 8-byte post ids is much smaller than rendered timelines.

Number of servers

servers = peak QPS ÷ QPS one server sustains, then add redundancy: at least one spare per availability zone so that losing a zone or deploying a release does not overload the rest. Per-server throughput depends wildly on the work done, so state your assumption. Rough orders of magnitude for planning:

Common mistake

Quoting per-server numbers as facts. They vary by an order of magnitude with workload. Say "assuming a server handles about 2,000 of these per second" so the interviewer can adjust it, and treat the resulting server count as a range. The useful output is "tens of servers" versus "thousands", not "35".

Worked example 1: a Twitter-like timeline

Assume 200 million DAU. Half of them post once a day, so 100 million tweets a day. Each user loads the home timeline 10 times a day. A stored tweet with metadata is about 300 bytes; a timeline page returns 20 tweets, about 6 KB before media. The average user has 200 followers.

~3.5k/stweet writes at peak
~69k/stimeline reads at peak
~231k/stimeline inserts if fanned out on write
~1.3 TBRAM for 800 cached ids per user
Writes 100M tweets/day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 1,157/s peak ×3 ≈ 3.5k/s Reads 200M × 10 = 2B/day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 23,148/s peak ×3 ≈ 69k/s Ratio reads : writes ≈ 20 : 1 Storage 100M × 300 B = 30 GB/day → × 365 × 5 × 3 replicas ≈ 164 TB of text Fan-out 100M tweets × 200 followers = 20B timeline inserts/day ≈ 231k/s Cache 200M users × 800 ids × 8 B ≈ 1.28 TB → a few dozen cache nodes Egress 69k/s × 6 KB ≈ 417 MB/s ≈ 3.3 Gbps, text only Servers 69k/s ÷ 2,000 per server ≈ 35, plus spares per zone

The conclusions: text storage is small (one modest cluster). The read rate requires serving timelines from memory. And the fan-out figure is the surprise: pushing each tweet into every follower's timeline costs about 231,000 cache writes per second on average, more than three times the peak read rate, and a celebrity with 50 million followers turns one tweet into 50 million writes. That single number motivates the hybrid push/pull design in the News Feed case study.

Worked example 2: photo storage

A photo-sharing service receives 10 million uploads a day. Each upload is stored as the original (about 1.7 MB) plus resized variants (about 300 KB total), so call it 2 MB per photo.

Ingest 10M × 2 MB = 20 TB/day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 231 MB/s ≈ 1.9 Gbps average upload Year 1 20 TB × 365 ≈ 7.3 PB raw 5 years flat rate: 36.5 PB with 50%/yr growth: 96.3 PB raw Replicated × 3 copies → ~290 PB erasure coded (× ~1.5) → ~145 PB Drives 145 PB ÷ 20 TB per drive ≈ 7,000+ drives before spares

The conclusions: this is an object storage problem, never a database one; the database holds only metadata (id, owner, object key, dimensions), about 1 KB per photo. The replication scheme is a cost decision worth tens of petabytes, which is why large stores use erasure coding for cold data. And the upload path should send bytes straight to the object store with presigned URLs, so 2 Gbps of uploads never passes through application servers.

Worked example 3: chat messages

A messaging app has 500 million DAU, each sending 40 messages a day. A message is about 100 bytes of text plus 100 bytes of metadata (ids, timestamps, status). At peak, 30% of DAU are connected at once.

Messages 500M × 40 = 20B/day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 231k/s peak ×3 ≈ 700k/s Storage 20B × 200 B = 4 TB/day ≈ 1.46 PB/year, before replication Connections 30% × 500M = 150M concurrent Gateways 150M ÷ 100k connections per server ≈ 1,500 servers (÷ 1M per tuned server ≈ 150)

The conclusions: the write rate (hundreds of thousands per second) rules out a single relational primary and points to a partitioned, write-optimised store keyed by conversation. Persistent connections, not request rate, size the gateway fleet, and the per-server connection limit swings the answer by ten times, so it is worth a sentence on memory per connection. WhatsApp famously reported over two million connections on one tuned server in 2012. Whether to keep history at all is a product question that changes storage from 4 TB a day kept forever to "until delivered". The Chat System case study builds on these numbers.

A parameterised helper

When practising, it helps to check your mental arithmetic. This helper takes the usual inputs and prints the numbers an interviewer expects. The output in the comment is from a real run with the timeline example's inputs.

import math

DAY = 86_400                       # seconds; use 10**5 when doing it in your head


def human(n, unit=""):
    for div, suffix in ((1e15, "P"), (1e12, "T"), (1e9, "G"), (1e6, "M"), (1e3, "k")):
        if n >= div:
            return f"{n / div:.1f} {suffix}{unit}"
    return f"{n:.0f} {unit}"


def estimate(dau, writes_per_user, reads_per_user, write_bytes, read_bytes,
             years=5, replicas=3, peak=3, per_server_rps=2_000, cache_fraction=0.2):
    w_avg = dau * writes_per_user / DAY
    r_avg = dau * reads_per_user / DAY
    stored_per_day = dau * writes_per_user * write_bytes
    servers = math.ceil(r_avg * peak / per_server_rps)
    return {
        "write QPS (avg / peak)": f"{w_avg:,.0f} / {w_avg * peak:,.0f}",
        "read QPS (avg / peak)": f"{r_avg:,.0f} / {r_avg * peak:,.0f}",
        "new data per day": human(stored_per_day, "B"),
        f"storage after {years} y x{replicas}": human(stored_per_day * 365 * years * replicas, "B"),
        "read egress at peak": human(r_avg * peak * read_bytes, "B/s"),
        "cache (20% of a day's reads)": human(dau * reads_per_user * read_bytes * cache_fraction, "B"),
        "read servers at peak": servers,
    }


# Twitter-like: 200M DAU, 0.5 tweets and 10 timeline loads per user per day,
# 300 B per tweet stored, a timeline page of 20 tweets returned (~6 KB)
for k, v in estimate(200e6, 0.5, 10, 300, 6_000).items():
    print(f"{k:30s} {v}")

# write QPS (avg / peak)         1,157 / 3,472
# read QPS (avg / peak)          23,148 / 69,444
# new data per day               30.0 GB
# storage after 5 y x3           164.2 TB
# read egress at peak            416.7 MB/s
# cache (20% of a day's reads)   2.4 TB
# read servers at peak           35

Notice the two cache figures for the same system: 2.4 TB if you cache 20% of rendered timeline pages, 1.28 TB if you cache 800 tweet ids per user and hydrate tweets from a separate, much smaller tweet cache. Choosing what to cache changed the answer by half; that is the kind of observation that earns credit.

Pitfalls and how to sound credible

PitfallBetter
False precision ("23,148.15 QPS")"About 23,000, call it 70,000 at peak." Round at every step
Mixing bits and bytesNetwork links are in bits per second; divide by 8 for bytes. A 10 Gbps link moves about 1.25 GB/s
Forgetting replication and overheadState "× 3 replicas" and "+ 50% for indexes" explicitly
Sizing for averageAlways apply a peak factor and say which one
Ignoring growthFor multi-year storage, ask about growth or assume one, and show both numbers
Estimating everythingCompute only the numbers that change a decision; skip the rest out loud
Numbers without conclusionsEnd each line with "so...": "so it fits in RAM", "so we need sharding"
Sanity checks

Compare against things you know. If your estimate says a chat app needs less storage than a single laptop, or more servers than Google, re-check the units. Off-by-1,000 errors (KB vs MB, per second vs per day) are far more common than wrong assumptions, and a quick comparison catches them.

Interview angle

When to bring it up

Right after requirements, for two to five minutes. Ask whether the interviewer wants it; some skip it. Estimate read and write QPS, storage, and anything else the design hinges on (connections for chat, egress for video, RAM for a cache). Then say the conclusions aloud.

Follow-ups to expect

  • "How many servers do you need?" Give the chain, the per-server assumption, and redundancy.
  • "Does it fit in memory?" Compute the hot set size and compare with RAM per node.
  • "What if traffic grows 10 times?" Show which numbers scale linearly and which component crosses a threshold first.
  • "Why is this request slow?" Reason from the latency ladder: count round trips and disk accesses.
  • "How much does the bandwidth cost?" Convert egress to TB per month; a CDN is usually the answer for media.

Red flags

  • Spending ten minutes on arithmetic, or doing long division on the board instead of rounding.
  • Getting units wrong by a factor of 1,000 and not noticing.
  • Sizing for average load only.
  • Numbers that never influence the design.
