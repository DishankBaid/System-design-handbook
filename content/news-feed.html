A news feed is the home timeline of Twitter, Instagram or LinkedIn: open the app and see recent posts from the accounts you follow, ranked, in under 200 ms, with infinite scroll. Producing it means combining posts from hundreds of authors per reader, for hundreds of millions of readers, while some authors have 100 million followers. The central decision is when that combining happens: when a post is written, when a feed is read, or a mix of both.

How to use this case study

Sections follow the order of a real interview. Concepts used here have their own chapters: Caching, Event Streaming, Distributed ID Generation, API Design (pagination), CDNs.

1. Requirements

Functional

Publish a post (text, images, video). Follow and unfollow. A home feed of posts from followed accounts, ranked by relevance, with infinite scroll. Like and comment, with visible counts. A profile page listing one author's posts.

Non-functional

Feed loads in under 200 ms p99. A new post appears in followers' feeds within a few seconds (eventual consistency is fine). Feed reads are far more frequent than posts. Highly available: a stale feed is acceptable, an empty one is not.

Out of scope

Search, direct messages, ads auction internals, content moderation pipelines. Mention them, then park them.

The key observation: reads outnumber writes by about 60 to 1, and the follower graph is extremely skewed. Most accounts have a few hundred followers; a few have tens of millions. A design that is optimal for the median author breaks for the celebrity, and vice versa.

2. Estimation

Assume 300 million daily active users who open the feed 10 times a day, 50 million new posts per day, and an average of 200 followers per author.

Fan-out on write turns 580 posts per second into 116,000 timeline writes per second. That is affordable for normal authors and catastrophic for one celebrity post, which alone would take the entire write capacity for over a minute and a half. The estimate makes the case for a hybrid before any diagram does.

The cursor is opaque on purpose. For a chronological feed it encodes the last post id returned ("older than 1840192837465001985"), which works because Snowflake-style ids sort by time. For a ranked feed it encodes a reference to a server-side ranked list plus an offset, so page 2 continues the same ranking instead of re-ranking and showing duplicates or skipping posts. Offset pagination (?page=3) is wrong in both cases: new posts arriving at the top shift every page down.

The timeline holds only ids. Post bodies live once in the post store and a post cache, and are hydrated (ids turned into full objects with author, media and counts) at read time. Storing full posts in every follower's timeline would multiply 50 GB of daily posts by 200 and make every edit and delete a 200-way rewrite.

5. High-level design

  1. Publish. The post service assigns a Snowflake id, writes the post to the post store and to the author's user_posts list, publishes post_created to Kafka, and returns. The author sees the post immediately from their own write.
  2. Fan out. Workers consume the event, page through the author's followers, and LPUSH plus LTRIM the post id onto each follower's timeline list, in pipelined batches of a few thousand. Authors above the celebrity threshold are skipped.
  3. Read. The feed service reads the viewer's timeline list, merges in recent posts from the celebrities they follow, hydrates the ids from the post cache with one multi-get, and sends roughly 1,500 candidates to ranking.
  4. Rank and page. The ranker scores candidates, filters and mixes them, and returns the first page of 20. The rest of the ranked list is cached for a few minutes under the cursor for later pages.

6. Deep dive: fan-out on write, on read, or both

This is the question the interview hinges on. Step through the three strategies with the same cast: Alice, a normal user with 300 followers, and a celebrity with 50 million. Bob follows both.

The hybrid is what Twitter described running for years: a Redis timeline per active user filled by fan-out, with high-follower accounts merged in at read time. Two refinements make it efficient:

  • Skip inactive users. Only push to followers who have opened the app in the last 30 days. A returning user's timeline is rebuilt on first read by pulling from their followees, which is slow once and fast afterwards. This often removes more than half of all fan-out writes.
  • A threshold, not a label. "Celebrity" means "more followers than the cutoff", for example 10,000. Tune it from the measured cost of a pushed write versus a read-time merge. The reader rarely follows more than a handful of accounts above the threshold, so the read side stays cheap.

Inside the timeline cache

Each active user's timeline is a Redis list of post ids (with the author id packed alongside, so read-time filters for blocks and mutes need no extra lookup). Fan-out appends with LPUSH tl:u_bob 1840192837465001985 followed by LTRIM tl:u_bob 0 799, pipelined so one round trip carries thousands of follower updates. Reads are LRANGE tl:u_bob 0 199. The cap of 800 bounds memory; almost nobody scrolls past a few hundred posts, and anyone who does falls through to a slower pull from the sources.

The cache is the only copy of the pushed timeline, but it is not the source of truth: the follow graph and the authors' post lists are. That makes losing a Redis node an availability event rather than a data-loss event. A missing timeline is rebuilt on read by pulling the latest posts of everyone the user follows (the fan-out-on-read path), written back to the cache, and served. Keep three replicas of each shard so that rebuilds are rare, and rate-limit rebuilds so a lost shard does not send millions of simultaneous pulls to the post store.

The read-time merge is a k-way merge of lists that are each sorted newest first, with the cursor applied to every source:

import heapq
from collections import defaultdict
from itertools import islice

CELEBRITY = 10_000      # followers above this: no fan-out on write
TIMELINE_CAP = 800      # entries kept per cached timeline

followers = defaultdict(set)
following = defaultdict(set)
posts_by = defaultdict(list)    # author -> own post ids, newest first
timeline = defaultdict(list)    # user -> pushed post ids, newest first


def follow(user, author):
    followers[author].add(user)
    following[user].add(author)


def publish(author, post_id):
    """post_id is a Snowflake-style id, so larger means newer."""
    posts_by[author].insert(0, post_id)
    if len(followers[author]) > CELEBRITY:
        return 0                                   # pulled at read time instead
    for f in followers[author]:                    # fan-out on write
        tl = timeline[f]
        tl.insert(0, post_id)
        del tl[TIMELINE_CAP:]
    return len(followers[author])


def home_feed(user, max_id=None, limit=4):
    """Merge the pushed timeline with celebrities' own lists. Cursor = last id returned."""
    celebs = [a for a in following[user] if len(followers[a]) > CELEBRITY]
    sources = [timeline[user]] + [posts_by[c] for c in celebs]
    below = ([p for p in s if max_id is None or p < max_id] for s in sources)
    page = list(islice(heapq.merge(*below, reverse=True), limit))
    return page, (page[-1] if page else None)


for i in range(12_000):                     # a celebrity with 12,000 followers
    follow(f"fan{i}", "celeb")
follow("bob", "alice")
follow("bob", "carol")
follow("bob", "celeb")

print(publish("alice", 7001), publish("celeb", 7002), publish("carol", 7003),
      publish("celeb", 7004), publish("alice", 7005), publish("carol", 7006))
# 1 0 1 0 1 1          (celebrity posts cost no fan-out writes)
page, cursor = home_feed("bob")
print(page, "next cursor:", cursor)
# [7006, 7005, 7004, 7003] next cursor: 7003
print(home_feed("bob", max_id=cursor))
# ([7002, 7001], 7001)  (celebrity posts 7004 and 7002 are merged in at read time)

7. Deep dive: the ranking pipeline

A chronological feed is a merge and a cut. A ranked feed is a funnel that narrows a large candidate set with progressively more expensive models, so that the costly model only scores what survives the cheap ones.

  • Candidate generation. Sources are the pushed timeline, celebrity pulls, and out-of-network recommendations (posts liked by people you follow, popular posts in your interests from an embedding nearest-neighbour index). Twitter's 2023 write-up describes fetching about 1,500 candidates per request this way.
  • Hydration and features. Batch-fetch post bodies, author data and precomputed features (author affinity, post age, engagement so far, media type). Features come from a feature store kept fresh by stream processing, never computed from raw logs at request time.
  • Light then heavy ranking. A cheap model (logistic regression or a small tree ensemble) prunes; a neural network predicts the probability of each engagement type, and a weighted sum of those probabilities is the score. Ranking runs in about 50 to 100 ms of the 200 ms budget, so it is the stage you cut first under load.
  • Filtering and mixing. Remove blocked and muted authors, already-seen posts and deleted posts. Enforce diversity (no more than two consecutive posts from one author), insert ads at fixed slots, and apply policy labels.
Degrade, do not fail

If the ranking service times out, return the merged candidates in reverse-chronological order. A plain feed in 150 ms is a much better outcome than a perfect feed in 3 seconds or an error page. Design the fallback in from the start and alert when it is being served.

8. Deep dive: pagination, counters and media

Cursor pagination

For the chronological part of the feed, the cursor is max_id: "give me posts older than 1840192837465001985". New posts arriving at the top cannot shift the window, and every source (timeline list, celebrity lists) can apply the same bound. For the ranked feed, page 1 computes the full ranked list of a few hundred ids and stores it under a short-lived session key such as rank:u_bob:9a1 with a 10-minute TTL; the cursor carries that key and an offset. Pages 2 to 20 are then just slices of a cached list: no re-ranking, no duplicates, and a fraction of the cost of the first page. A pull-to-refresh starts a new session, and posts newer than the session's first item are shown as "12 new posts" at the top.

Counters for likes

Like counts are written far more than posts and are extremely skewed: a post from a celebrity can collect 40,000 likes per second in its first minutes. A single counter row or key becomes a write hotspot. Two complementary fixes:

  • Sharded counters, as above, for the live count in the cache tier.
  • Write-behind aggregation for the durable count: each like inserts a (post_id, user_id) row (which makes it idempotent) and emits an event; a stream job sums events per post per second and applies one UPDATE ... SET like_count = like_count + 4812. The database sees one write per hot post per second instead of tens of thousands.

The displayed number is allowed to lag by a few seconds and to be slightly off. "18.4K likes" never needs to be exact, but "liked by me" does, and it comes from the likes table, not from the counter.

Media

Clients upload images and videos directly to blob storage with presigned URLs; the post only references media_ids. A processing pipeline produces several sizes (thumbnail, feed width, full screen, in modern formats such as WebP or AVIF) and the feed returns URLs on the CDN. At 5 TB a day of new media, the CDN serves almost all media bytes and the origin sees mostly first requests.

10. Key trade-offs to state

  • Hybrid fan-out: cheap reads and bounded writes, in exchange for two code paths and a tuned threshold.
  • Ids in timelines, bodies hydrated: one copy of each post, easy edits and deletes, in exchange for a batched lookup on every read.
  • Eventual consistency: a post may take seconds to reach followers, which nobody notices; it buys an asynchronous, retryable fan-out.
  • Ranked over chronological: higher engagement, in exchange for a latency-heavy pipeline that needs a fallback and makes pagination stateful.
  • Approximate counters: write scalability for hot posts, in exchange for displayed counts that lag by seconds.
Interview angle

What the interviewer is checking

Whether you derive fan-out costs from numbers (followers per post, reads per day), spot the celebrity problem yourself, and land on a hybrid with a clear threshold. Senior candidates also cover pagination stability, hydration and the ranking fallback.

Follow-ups to expect

  • A user with 100 million followers posts. Walk me through what happens.
  • How does a user who has been inactive for a year get a feed?
  • How do you paginate a ranked feed without duplicates when new posts arrive?
  • A post is deleted. How do you make it disappear from 5 million timelines?
  • How do you keep like counts correct for a viral post?
  • Ranking is slow today. What does the user see?

Red flags

  • Pure fan-out on write with no plan for celebrities, or pure fan-out on read with no plan for read cost.
  • Copying full post bodies into every timeline.
  • Offset-based pagination for a constantly changing list.
  • A single counter row updated on every like.
